Every event in your account has to learn from scratch. That is the constraint underneath most of what you listed, and it is worth fixing before you add markets.

Meta's roughly 50 conversions a week is measured per ad set, per optimization event, on a rolling seven days. One campaign per event means four concurrent events are four separate failures to reach it, not one portfolio that clears it. Run twenty events and you would produce roughly 160 to 320 purchases a month, genuinely enough signal to optimize on. It is just split into twenty pieces that never inform each other. Consolidation is not housekeeping here. It is the only route to ever having optimization data.

What breaks first is not budget or delivery. It is that you pay the learning cost twice per city, once going in and again every time you return, while the pixel never builds a durable picture of who buys a beginner clinic. Auction overlap between neighbouring markets comes later and is easier to fix.

Second disagreement, stated plainly. Your ad metrics are not the problem. Cheap clicks and strong click-through into a page where most visitors never reach the middle, and only a fraction reach checkout, is the signature of a page and offer not carrying what the ad promised. Media buying can move that a little by changing who arrives. It cannot fix it. I would expect your constraints to rank offer and page first, structure second, media third, and would want to read your Clarity recordings against the ad that drove each session first.

**1. Learning phase, and what to optimize for.**

Stop chasing it per event and stop optimizing new cities for purchase. Consolidate so signal aggregates across markets instead of resetting per event, which is what makes the threshold reachable at portfolio level. Then move the optimization event up the funnel to something firing ten to twenty times more often than a purchase, and keep purchase as the metric you judge on rather than the one you bid on. You optimize for the highest frequency event that still correlates with a sale, and hold it accountable on cost per seat. The mistake at your volume is letting the same number be both the target and the evidence.

**2. A local business across multiple markets.**

The gap first, because it is what you screened for. Our book does not contain a ticketed, fixed-date, multi-city event business. If that exact pattern is the requirement, we are not the closest firm you will talk to and you should weigh that accordingly.

The nearest real analog our team runs is a certification programme sold to adults nationally on Meta, where the win came out of account structure rather than budget. Cost per acquisition fell 52 percent, from $256 to $123, and monthly applications rose from 41 to 90 on a 5 percent spend increase. That is the mechanism your account needs, consolidating fragmented learning rather than buying traffic. Our team has also run a local experiential business at roughly $27 a day, so the small-budget constraint is familiar.

What we would do differently: consolidate far earlier than felt comfortable. The instinct to keep campaigns separate so results stay readable is what starves them.

**3. A brand new city, first 72 hours.**

Treat it as audience discovery, not selling seats. Launch into the consolidated structure so the market inherits what the pixel already knows rather than opening an isolated campaign. Go broad instead of stacking a tight radius on a cold market, since narrow geo plus low budget is what produces the expensive outliers. Optimize on the upper funnel event, and hold creative constant against what works elsewhere so the only new variable is the market.

Then judge on indicators that have volume in 72 hours, cost per landing page view and the rate those visitors reach checkout, benchmarked against your portfolio norms rather than against zero. Purchases in the first three days are close to meaningless, and acting on them is how good markets get killed. A market is failing when reach is cheap and the checkout rate sits well below baseline, because that says the offer is not landing. It needs patience when the checkout rate is normal and volume is simply thin.

Launch earlier than a two to three week window too. The first several days are learning, which leaves little selling time, and a 40-plus buyer weighing a $200 to $300 experience is not an impulse purchase. Four to six weeks, with a low-budget warming phase before scaling, fits that decision better.

Retargeting is the other thing to build first, ahead of lookalikes and any warm audience work. You send traffic to a page most visitors leave, and nothing follows them.
