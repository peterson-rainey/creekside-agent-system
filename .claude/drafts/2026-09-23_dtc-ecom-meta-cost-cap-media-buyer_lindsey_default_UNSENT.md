Most cost-cap problems get diagnosed off the cost column. That is the wrong column. A cap set below what the auction will clear does not overspend, it quietly stops buying, and it reports a flattering cost per result while it does it.

I will say up front that I cannot hand you a cost-cap account as proof, and I come back to that below. What I can show you is the delivery pattern that starvation leaves behind, because it looks much the same whatever is throttling the spend. On a DTC ecommerce account in our book I went back through 34 campaigns and $58,298 spent between February 2 and June 15 this year, and sorted them by how steadily they actually spent rather than by what they returned.

The steadiest campaigns never recorded a zero-spend day. One broad purchase campaign ran 39 straight days, with daily spend varying about 16 percent around its own average, at a $17.96 CPM and $62.06 per recorded conversion. A second ran 46 straight days, 20 percent variation, $18.81 CPM, $65.65.

The erratic ones went the other way. One CBO campaign delivered on 6 of its 11 days, varied 114 percent around its average, and paid a $51.70 CPM and $158.17 per conversion. Another delivered on 8 of 11 days at a $44.84 CPM and $107.31.

Three things to be honest about before that reads as proof. The steady campaigns ran in March and April and the erratic ones in May and June, so seasonality is not ruled out. The erratic ones were scaling pushes averaging roughly $200 to $320 a day against $70 to $150, and higher daily spend bids higher on its own. And those are conversions as the platform recorded them, not verified purchases, on an account where under 40 percent of delivery days carried a recorded conversion at all, so treat the levels as rough and the gap between them as the point.

The gaps themselves are the real question. A zero-spend day can mean a cap that stopped clearing or a campaign somebody paused. Telling those two apart is the whole diagnostic, and it is answered by pacing per ad set per day against the cap, not by the CPA chart.

Structured creative testing and DTC ecommerce on Meta are well covered in our book, across meal prep, replacement parts, furniture and consumer hardware. A documented cost-cap track record is not. The one bid-cap campaign I can point to spent $16 and never delivered, which taught me the starvation pattern above and nothing else. If a proven cost-cap operator is the hard requirement, I am not going to dress that up for you.

Fatigue I read as frequency, CPM and cost per result together rather than click-through on its own. Click decay is a weak trigger, and swapping creative on it resets learning that the cap then has to re-earn, which is how a healthy account gets talked into a bad month.

For tooling it is the platform reporting plus your backend numbers, and I query the ad account directly when the reporting needs rebuilding, which is how the figures above were produced. Reporting on our side runs every two weeks alongside a live dashboard.
