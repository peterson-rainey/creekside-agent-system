## Answer (paste-ready)

The mix is the part that lines up. I run nine Meta accounts at once right now, and they are not variations on one business. Some are local practices selling appointments, where a good week is measured in booked consultations and the ceiling is how many calls the front desk can return. Others are ecommerce brands selling physical product, where the same week is measured in return on ad spend and the ceiling is inventory and creative. Nine accounts, nine different definitions of a good week, and the mistake I see most in a group is one playbook stretched across all of them.

The deepest recent example is a dental practice I ran cold Meta lead forms for earlier this year. Over 78 active days it spent about $65,000 and produced 2,558 leads at $25.31 each. I added qualifying questions to the forms on purpose and accepted a higher cost per lead in exchange, because leads the phone team could actually work were worth more than cheap ones stacking up in a queue. Then we throttled spend down deliberately, not because the economics broke but because the call center could not keep up with what was already coming in. In May their CRM attributed a little under $500,000 of revenue to that pipeline, not all of which I would claim, but it is why lead quality mattered more there than cost per lead. They eventually took the whole thing in house and kept the structure.

I will walk you through finished accounts like that one in as much detail as you want. Live client results I keep off a public proposal, and you would want the same once you are the live client.

Ten plus years of this, and I built and sold my own e-commerce business, so the operator side of a group feels familiar rather than new.
