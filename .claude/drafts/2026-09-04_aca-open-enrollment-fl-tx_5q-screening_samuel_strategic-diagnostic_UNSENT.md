1. Describe one U.S.-based lead-generation campaign you personally managed.

A multi-location dental implant group in California, Meta lead forms feeding a call center that qualified and booked every lead. That handoff is the closest thing in our book to what you have described with your internal assistant, which is why I am leading with it rather than something with a prettier number.

From April 21 to July 15 this year the account spent $64,746.78 and produced 2,558 leads at $25.31 blended, across eleven campaigns. Nine were English and two were Spanish, built separately rather than translated, and the Spanish campaigns came in at $17.93 against $26.38 for English. Our team ran Meta on that account; I am not going to claim I was the hands-on buyer.

The useful part is not the cost per lead. Partway through, spend was pulled back, and the reporting made it look like an ad problem. It was not. The call center could not work the volume, and contact rate and set rate were not being reported next to cost per lead, so nobody could see that for weeks. Any funnel with a human step downstream has a real capacity ceiling, and if you do not measure it, you will misread a throughput problem as a media problem. The engagement ended in July when they took marketing in house.

2. Have you worked with ACA, health insurance, Medicare, healthcare, or another regulated U.S. industry?

Healthcare and legal yes, insurance no.

Healthcare is the bulk of our regulated work: the implant group above, a cosmetic dentistry practice where cost per consultation went from $48.79 to $9.58, a naturopathic practice running $14 to $40 per patient conversion, and a GLP-1 telehealth account. Legal: a bankruptcy firm in Orange County where leads went from 117 to 229 while cost per acquisition fell from $86 to $50.29.

No ACA, health insurance or Medicare account, and no health certification on either platform. What I can tell you unprompted is the constraint that will shape this build. Since January 2025 Meta classifies every insurance product, health included, under the Financial Products and Services special ad category. Advertiser identity verification and proof of licensing gate launch before a single ad runs, and inside the category age locks to 18 to 65 plus while household income, detailed demographics, lookalikes and exclusions all disappear, with a 15 mile minimum radius and no ZIP. ACA eligibility is essentially income, household size and age, which is exactly what Meta takes away. I know the platform terrain. I have not carried an account through a CMS compliance review, and if that is a requirement rather than a preference you should weigh it against the rest of this.

3. What is your experience managing both Meta Ads and Google Ads for lead generation?

Six years across both, currently around 16 live Google accounts and about a quarter million a month of Meta spend. Google Search and Meta are the two things we sell, so this is the whole business rather than a side capability.

For your campaign the split is not a preference, it follows from the rules above. Google's restricted targeting policy covers housing, employment and credit, not insurance, so on Google you keep ZIP level geography, remarketing and Customer Match, all of which Meta removes. That makes Google the precision instrument for Florida county level work and the place to capture intent that only exists once enrollment opens. Meta is where you build consideration before November 1 and where the Spanish audience is cheapest to reach. Roughly 60 Google and 40 Meta once enrollment opens, inverted through October, then let the qualification data move it rather than holding a fixed percentage.

4. What cost per lead, and if available cost per qualified lead or customer, did you achieve?

Cost per lead precisely: $25.31 blended on the implant account across 2,558 leads, $26.38 English and $17.93 Spanish. On the bankruptcy firm, $50.29 per lead on search, down from $86.

Cost per qualified lead I cannot give you honestly. In nearly all of these engagements qualification happened inside the client's own CRM and that outcome never came back to the ad platform, so cost per lead is what we captured reliably. I would rather say that than hand you a number I cannot stand behind.

That gap is the specific reason your setup is interesting. You already have someone qualifying, scheduling and reporting on lead quality. Written back as an offline conversion, that status stops being something reconstructed by hand at month end and becomes the event the campaigns optimize toward. On the implant account the two things that actually moved economics both came from the call center rather than the ad account: qualifying questions added to the form, accepted as a higher cost per lead in exchange for better leads, and spend throttled to match capacity. I would want that feedback wired in the first few weeks, not at the end.

5. What would you charge for initial setup and ongoing monthly management?

Setup is a one-time onboarding fee of $1,500 per platform, so $3,000 for Meta and Google together. That covers account structure, tracking and conversion setup, audience and keyword research, and the campaign build.

Ongoing management is a percentage of ad spend rather than hourly: 20 percent up to $30,000 a month, 15 percent on the next $30,000, 10 percent above that, with a $1,500 per platform monthly minimum. The tiers are marginal, so the fee does not scale linearly as budget grows.

For an initial Florida test I would recommend $12,000 to $15,000 a month across both platforms, and the reason is learning phase rather than appetite. Meta wants roughly 50 conversions per ad set per week to optimize reliably, so at a $25 to $60 lead a single ad set needs $1,250 to $3,000 a week on its own, and you want two live plus Google beside them. Under about $10,000 you spend Open Enrollment collecting noise. At $12,000 split across the two platforms the management fee is $3,000 a month.

Ad spend stays paid by you directly to Meta and Google, separate from the management fee, as you specified.
