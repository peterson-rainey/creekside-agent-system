Bidding on a competitor's name as a keyword is allowed. Putting it in your ad text is the part Google restricts, and its policy names that case exactly: use in an ad from a direct competitor. That is Google's line, not a trademark owner's, which matters if you resell a brand you would be conquesting. Enforcement waits on a complaint, and restrictions then generally apply to any ad pointing at that root domain. The strategy survives. The ad copy is where the ceiling sits.

The competitor campaigns in our book have gone both ways, which is the useful part. In a dental implant account this year, the competitor campaign brought leads at $64 against the brand campaign's $137 over an overlapping window, while a second geo there spent $382 and converted nobody. In a yarn and tufting store, the competitor campaign returned 1.73x while Shopping and PMax off the product catalogue did 3.0x to 7.6x, on a small $466 test.

What splits them seems to be whether the searcher is choosing a supplier or a product. Someone typing a competitor clinic's name has not committed. Someone typing a store's brand name usually wants that store's item, so you are buying into a decision already made. Worth testing on your own search terms before assuming it holds for yours.

Meta is harder to copy from than people expect. The Ad Library keeps all active ads but retains stopped ones only where they delivered to the UK or EU, and spend and results are never shown. If the competitors you have in mind also deliver there, their full history is in the open and worth pulling first. If not, you see what is live today and nothing about what got switched off.

For launch, the product feed carries order volume, with competitor targeting beside it on a capped slice, killed if it has not beaten blended cost per order by month two. Three ecommerce brands in our book run both platforms that way right now.
