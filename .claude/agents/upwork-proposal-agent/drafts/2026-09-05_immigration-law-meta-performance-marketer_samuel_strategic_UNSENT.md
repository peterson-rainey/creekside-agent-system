## Draft (paste as-is)

QUALIFIED LEADS

Immigration is one of the few verticals where a cheap Meta lead is actively dangerous. The optimizer only learns from the event you send it, and if the only event firing is "form submitted," it will go find you more people who enjoy filling out forms. On immigration that means volume from people with no viable case, no ability to fund a retainer, or a matter you don't practice. CPL drops, intake drowns, and the account looks like it's winning right up until someone counts signed clients.

The fix isn't creative, it's the event you optimize on. Two-stage tracking: a shallow event on form fill, then a separate custom event that only fires once intake marks the lead viable. Send both to Meta, optimize on the second one. That's the same architecture I built for a reverse mortgage client — a pre-qualified event at the top and a downstream qualified event that intake controlled — and it's what makes the lead-quality feedback loop real instead of a monthly conversation.

Second thing I'd push on early: practice areas don't belong in one campaign. Family-based petitions, removal defense, and employment visas have completely different urgency, ticket size, and message. Pooled together, the cheapest one eats the budget and skews everything.

**Law firms.** Personally, my legal work has been Google, not Meta — bankruptcy for a California firm where we doubled leads (117 to 229) and cut CPA from $86 to $50.29 through market-level restructuring, plus personal injury for a multi-office firm. **Closest Meta proof.** High-ticket dental implants: $64,747 spend, 2,558 leads at $25.31, running into a call-center intake team. Same shape as immigration — expensive consult-driven service, lead forms, a human team qualifying every lead.

**Good CPL, bad quality.** That dental account is the story. Leads were cheap and volume was real, but the constraint turned out to be intake capacity, not cost — the team couldn't work the volume fast enough, so speed-to-lead collapsed and quality looked worse than it was. Fixes were qualifying questions on the form to slow it down deliberately, tighter geo, and pacing spend to what intake could actually absorb in a day.

**Who owns creative performance.** The media buyer. The creative team owns craft; if a concept underperforms, that's a brief problem, which is mine. I'd write briefs specific enough that your video and design people are executing a hypothesis, not guessing at a vibe.

**CRM data.** By pushing qualified/consult/retained status back into Meta as offline conversions and matching on click ID, so the algorithm optimizes toward the outcome instead of the form.
