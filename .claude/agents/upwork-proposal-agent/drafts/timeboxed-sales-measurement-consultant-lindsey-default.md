Are the listing campaigns already declared under Meta's housing special ad category, or has Meta not caught up yet? A campaign flagged mid flight resets learning, and on something that closes in a few weeks there is no runway to earn that back.

Inside that category the targeting toolkit is mostly gone. No age or gender, no detailed demographics or interests, no lookalikes since special ad audiences were retired in 2022, and a 15 mile minimum radius, so ZIP level is out. Google's housing policy works the same way. So the measurement problem and the targeting problem are the same problem. Strip targeting out and the only levers left are creative, the optimization event, and how the form qualifies. None of the three can be steered unless the data separates a qualified outcome from a raw form fill, per listing rather than pooled.

A two to four week campaign can also finish without ever leaving the learning phase, and a fresh campaign per listing restarts that clock every time, so carrying learning across listings is the real edge on countdown pacing. On an evergreen account a measurement error is recoverable. With a close date it is just gone, which is why the audit lands before the pilot, not beside it.

I ran the Meta side of a reverse mortgage lender inside a special ad category, about $147K over twelve months, so the restriction set is familiar even though the vertical is not. The honest gap: I have not personally owned property listings, so the definition of a qualified outcome comes from you, not from me assuming it.

Ten years on Meta, plus an ecommerce brand I built and sold. Largest account I run right now is just under $100K a month, and I work in it myself. Same hands on the audit as on whatever follows.

## Portfolio: three campaigns I personally owned

A reverse mortgage lender, Meta, roughly $147K in spend from September 2025 through August 2026, peaking just under $22K in a single month. The whole engagement ran inside a special ad category, same restriction set as housing. What I measured was cost per lead against lead volume. The cleanest stretch was April into May, where CPL went from $82.46 to $59.33 while volume went up, 109 leads to 140, on slightly lower spend. What I would change: reporting had no conversion data at all for seven of the twelve months, which is why I quote that window and not a blended figure. June ran to $846.63 CPL on fifteen leads, and I found it at month end instead of inside the first week. Both of those are the same failure, which is that I inherited the measurement setup and worked around it instead of rebuilding it in week one.

A dental group, Meta lead forms feeding a call center, about $64.7K from April through July 2026. Platform reported 2,558 leads at $25.31 blended. The outcome that actually mattered was what the call center could qualify, and the gap between those two numbers was never instrumented, so I was optimizing toward a number I could not fully defend. That is the miss, and it is the one most relevant to your post. What I would change is closing that loop before scaling anything. I also took spend from about $9K to $32.5K inside a single month and CPL went from $18.74 to $29.85. It recovered to $25.48 and then $20.06 as creative caught up, but I paid for that gap rather than staging the increase.

A parts retailer, Meta, holding roughly $12K to $15.7K a month. Measured on CPA and CPM. Between October and December 2025 CPM went from $8.06 to $11.68 and CPA from $27.50 to $44.93, at essentially flat spend. It snapped back to $8.10 CPM in January, and the best month was April 2026 at $8.18 CPM and $19.89 CPA on 665 conversions. What I would change: I held spend flat through the expensive stretch on the assumption the auction would normalize, and it did. But that was waiting it out, not managing it. Now I would pull budget forward into the cheap months and pull back through the expensive ones. That account could afford to ride the calendar. A campaign with a hard close date cannot, which is the part of countdown pacing that catches people out.
