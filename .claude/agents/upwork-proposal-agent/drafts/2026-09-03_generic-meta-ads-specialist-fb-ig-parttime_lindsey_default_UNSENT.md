## PROPOSAL (paste-ready)

Have you counted how many edits a Meta campaign takes in an average week? Asking because on a brief built around monitoring and adjusting, that count has told me more than the dashboard does.

Not every change costs you, but the ones that do are the ones people make most: audience edits, a new creative in a live ad set, a swap of the optimization event or bid strategy, a budget move past twenty percent. Each puts the ad set back into learning. A weekly reporting rhythm quietly invites a weekly change, so the account spends most of its life re-learning and never sits still long enough to give a clean read. The test that would have told you something gets cut on day four for looking soft. Separating how often you report from how often you touch the account is usually the first fix, and it costs nothing.

Over ten years in, and I built and sold my own e-commerce brand before that, which taught me to sit through a number I did not like. I ran cold Meta lead forms into a call team for a dental implant practice, 2,558 leads at $25.31 each over about three months. A spa parts store held around 5.5x blended across its final nine months with me. Budget range surprises people: single Meta accounts on my desk run from roughly $3,000 a month up into six figures, and one has sat near the top of it for twelve months straight. Structure that works at one end breaks quietly at the other.

Two things worth settling before anyone hands you a plan: roughly what monthly range you are working with, and which countries you are selling into.

There is a short video on my profile covering how I work accounts like this.

**`expert-review-agent`: three findings accepted, four rejected.**

Accepted:
1. *The learning-phase claim was oversimplified.* "Every meaningful change puts an ad set back into learning" is
   closer to 2019-era Meta than 2026. Replaced with the actual reset triggers (audience edits, new creative in a
   live ad set, optimization-event or bid-strategy swap, budget move past twenty percent), which is both correct
   and better proof of expertise, which the style doc requires.
2. *The preview line did not name the platform.* A buyer sees only the first sentence or two before deciding to
   click, and the original opener was platform-agnostic. "Meta" now appears in the first sentence.
3. *The opening assertion was stated as a universal.* Reworded to "that count has told me more than the dashboard
   does", grounding it in her own experience rather than claiming a general law.

Rejected, with reasons:
1. *"Ten years contradicts the 6-years canon."* Wrong profile. Six years is Samuel. The canonical
   `lindsey-default.md` states "10+ years" and "built and sold her own e-commerce business" as her primary
   credibility anchor. The reviewer flagged its own uncertainty here.
2. *"Add a call CTA."* lindsey_default closes on the profile-video line by design, and they offered no call.
3. *"Address hours-per-week availability."* Lindsey's actual weekly capacity is an open unknown (it is the live
   blocker on the pet-brands lead). Claiming a number would be inventing one.
4. *"Never touches campaign setup or audience targeting."* Partly fair, but the post carries zero required items,
   lindsey_default is experience-first rather than a scope checklist, and reciting their own deliverable list back
   at them is the parroting failure mode. Budget management, reporting cadence, optimization and testing are all
   addressed on their merits; setup and targeting are not, deliberately.

One expert note recorded but not acted on: citing a near-six-figure account against a part-time ask can read as
overqualification. The range framing ("from roughly $3,000 a month up into six figures") already shows both ends,
which is the defusal.
