## 1. How do you use metrics to inform your strategy?

The metric I report and the metric I cut on are rarely the same one, and keeping those separate is most of what I actually do.

The clearest example I have is a replacement parts brand I ran on Meta. Through last Q4 its blended return fell from 6.45x in October to 3.22x in December. Nothing about the creative had gotten worse. Click through rate rose across those same three months, 1.59% to 1.86%, while CPM went up about 45%. Return was measuring the auction. Click through was measuring the ads. Cutting on return there would have killed the angles that were working hardest, right before media got cheap again.

So the first thing I ask of a metric is what it is measuring right now, and the second is what would have to be true for it to be lying to me.

Two habits come out of that. The first is that I do not accept a rate without its denominator. On that same account, four campaigns optimizing to shallow events all showed better returns than the broad purchase campaign, one of them at 10.29x. It was running about $3,300. The purchase campaign sat at 5.02x on nearly $67,000. The flattering numbers were sitting where the money was not, which is not a finding, it is arithmetic.

The second is that I name the control before I claim a result. When that account recovered from 4.57x in January to 8.07x in April, the honest version includes that CPM was flat across that window, $8.10 against $8.18, and frequency was flat as well. That is what makes it a real read rather than cheap media doing the work. It also means I will tell you when a number moved for reasons that had nothing to do with me. Part of that climb out of December was Q4 pricing unwinding, not anything I did.

## 2. Describe your recent experience with similar projects

Straight answer first. The paid management side of what you described is the strongest thing on my list and the whitelisting piece is the weakest, so here is which is which.

Closest match is a replacement parts e-commerce brand on Meta, mine day to day for about ten months until it wrapped this past May. Roughly $130,000 in spend, around 5.5x blended, 4,481 orders. That engagement was largely the work in your post: reading what the account was telling me early and moving budget and creative on it. The clearest stretch was consolidating fourteen campaigns down to seven and taking cost per acquisition from $44.93 in December to $19.89 by April, on monthly spend that came down rather than up.

For scaling a brand, a luxury furniture DTC account, also Meta, also mine. Monthly spend went from about $12,000 to $40,389 across four months while blended return went from 1.65x to 4.17x. Two caveats I would rather hand you now than have you find later. That 4.17x month was carried by a sitewide 50 percent promotion and by retargeting, so it is not a cold acquisition number. And the account churned not long after.

The largest account I run is a cosmetic and aesthetic practice, live right now, just under $99,000 a month and about $1.19 million over the last twelve months. That is services rather than a product brand, so I offer it as evidence of holding spend at volume and nothing more than that.

On whitelisted and partnership driven campaigns, I have not run those at volume. I understand the mechanism and I am not going to dress that up into experience I do not have.

On pixel and launch readiness, I would want to see what you already have before saying what I would change. The first thing I do on any account before it spends is confirm that the events firing are the ones being optimized toward, because everything downstream inherits that choice.
