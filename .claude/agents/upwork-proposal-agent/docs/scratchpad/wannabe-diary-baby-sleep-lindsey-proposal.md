## PROPOSAL BODY

GENTLE SCALE

When you say a blended ROAS around 2, does that number include your existing customers, or is it cold acquisition only? With thousands of buyers and a 200K organic audience behind you, that one distinction usually decides whether scaling works or quietly stops working.

The reason I ask is that repeat buyers and warm retargeting sit inside most blended numbers, and they are the cheapest conversions in any account. A brand can hold a 2 blended while cold traffic runs at a loss, and the moment you add budget, the loss is the part that scales. The warm pool does not grow with spend. It is a fixed asset you are already harvesting.

So the first thing I would look at is whether your new-customer campaigns hard-exclude previous purchasers. I run a meal prep brand on exactly that structure right now. Low ticket, bought repeatedly, bumps and upsells on the back end, which is structurally the same acquisition problem you have. Cost per new customer went from about $11.50 to $5.50 over six months while monthly volume roughly tripled on a nearly flat budget. It is sitting closer to $10 this month after a creative set fatigued, which is honestly the more useful thing to know about how I work.

Before that I ran a replacement parts ecommerce brand for ten months at roughly $13K a month. Reported return fell from 7.7x to 4.3x through a fourth quarter and every instinct said the creative had broken. It had not. Click through was climbing the whole way, 1.59% to 1.86%, while CPMs rose about 45%. Cost per conversion went from $44.93 in December to $19.89 by April on flat CPMs.

Ten years on Meta, plus an ecommerce brand I built and sold, mostly taught me that scaling is about not killing what already works.

Attached a few results below so you can see what this looks like in practice.

### 3. Creative testing when the brand already has a large organic library.

Organic performance is a shortlist, not a verdict. A reel that did 400K views has earned a place in the test queue. It has not proven it can sell to a stranger who never chose to follow you.

1. Pull 90 days of organic and rank by hold rate and saves, not views. Views measure distribution. Holds and saves measure interest.
2. Sort that shortlist by the promise each piece makes, not by format. Usually four or five distinct promises hide inside thirty pieces of content, and testing thirty assets that all say the same thing is one test run expensively.
3. Test at promise level first. One broad campaign, three to five ad sets, one promise each, enough budget to reach roughly 50 conversions before I read anything.
4. Only after a promise wins do I test format inside it. Talking head against text-on-screen against customer story.
5. Hooks last. Hook testing on a losing promise is the most common waste I see in accounts like yours.

For a 4 to 18 month offer I would expect the promises to split by parent state rather than baby age. The exhausted parent who has stopped functioning and the parent who is philosophically against cry-it-out are two different buyers, and the age brackets cut across both.

### 4. Good CTR, low CPC, still not profitable. What do you analyze first?

Tracking, before anything else. Cheap clicks and no profit is the signature of an account where the purchase event is double counting, firing wrong, or not firing at all on a bump or an upsell. I reconcile pixel and CAPI counts against actual checkout counts for the same window before touching a single campaign.

If tracking is clean, the next question is which promise those cheap clicks are responding to. High CTR at low CPC usually means the creative is winning attention with something the page does not deliver. Curiosity hooks do this constantly. The click is cheap because the ad is interesting, and the sale never happens because the visitor arrived expecting a different thing.

Third is traffic mix. Cheap clicks often mean placement drift into Audience Network or heavy Reels distribution, and those convert at a fraction of feed traffic. That is a delivery problem wearing a creative problem's clothes.

Fourth is the economics. If CTR and CPC are genuinely healthy and tracking is clean, then the CAC your funnel can support is lower than the CAC the auction is charging, and the fix is the offer, the bump take rate or the AOV. Not the ads. I would rather tell you that in week two than spend three months optimizing toward a number the funnel cannot carry.

### 5. Increase budget, duplicate, iterate, or stop?

Data volume first. I do not act on an ad set with under 50 conversions in the window unless it is spending fast and producing nothing.

- **Increase budget** when cost per new customer is at or under target and has held there at least 7 days with stable frequency. Up 20 to 30 percent at a time, never doubles. Doubling resets learning and you pay for the same education twice.
- **Duplicate** when an ad set is a genuine winner and I want more volume without disturbing a stable delivery pattern. Duplication adds capacity. It does not fix a loser. Duplicating a bad ad set is superstition.
- **Iterate creative** when CTR is holding but frequency is climbing and cost is drifting up. That is fatigue. The promise still works and the execution is worn out, so it is a new hook on the same angle.
- **Stop** when the promise is wrong, not when the number is bad on a Tuesday. Concretely: 2 to 3x target CAC spent with no conversion, or CTR materially below account average from the start. Rejections should be fast.

The meal prep account I mentioned is in an iterate phase right now, not a stop phase, which is why I have not pulled it apart.
