When Meta rejects one of your mortgage ads, is it the ad copy getting flagged or the landing page behind it? Most accounts assume copy, and often enough it is the destination, a missing disclosure or a headline the page never qualifies. Meta reviews where the click lands, and mortgage is one of the categories where it is strict about it.

That distinction matters more than usual here, because under the financial products and services category copy is not only a compliance surface, it is your only real targeting lever. Age locked to a broad band, no gender, no household income or detailed demographics, no lookalikes, no exclusions, fifteen mile minimum radius. You cannot target your way to a better lead. Qualification has to live in the creative, the form, and the event you optimize against. Which means an account that gets gun shy after a few rejections and starts writing vaguer ads quietly makes its lead quality worse. Rejections and lead quality are usually the same problem wearing two hats.

Our team ran Meta for a reverse mortgage lender for about a year, roughly $149K through the account, peaking just under $22K in a month, all of it inside that category. The stretch I would point at is April into May this year, where cost per lead went from $82 to $59 while volume went up, 109 leads to 140, on slightly lower spend. Targeting was not one of the levers available, so that came out of creative and the optimization event.

On the rejection side, every ad cleared a written checklist before it published, built from the lender's own compliance requirements and the NRMLA advertising rules. No implied government endorsement, no free money framing, no guaranteed approval, benefit claims balanced against what the borrower still owes. Once that was a step in the process instead of a reaction to a disapproval, rejections got rare.

Two things worth knowing before I say anything concrete about structure. Roughly where does monthly spend sit now, and is scaling closer to adding twenty percent or doubling it? And which markets do the ads serve, since the category rules bite differently by country and that changes what is actually fixable.

Happy to walk the account on a quick call.

## Screening Q: Describe your recent experience with similar projects

The closest match is a reverse mortgage lender we ran Meta for from August last year through this August, when the engagement ended. Roughly $149K in Meta spend across the run, peaking just under $22K in a month, all of it inside the financial products and services category.

On CPL, the cleanest window was April into May this year. Cost per lead went from $82 to $59 while volume went up, 109 leads to 140, on slightly lower spend. Because the category takes targeting off the table, that came out of creative and the optimization event rather than audience work. I will be straight with you that conversion reporting on that account had gaps across the year, so I am quoting the window I can stand behind rather than a blended number for the whole engagement.

On rejections, every ad cleared a written checklist before it published, built from the lender's own compliance requirements and the NRMLA advertising rules. No implied government endorsement, no free money or stimulus framing, no guaranteed approval, benefit claims balanced against the borrower's obligation to keep paying taxes, insurance and maintenance. The traps here are specific. On the search side of that same account, their compliance officer caught an ad that had blended two products, the lender's proprietary 55+ product with the FHA reverse mortgage at 62+. Same brand, same account, two different eligibility ages, and it could not be fixed with a blind find and replace because half the instances were correct. That is the kind of thing that quietly generates disapprovals, and a generalist does not catch it.

For scale, the nearest comparison is a dental group we ran Meta lead forms into a call center for, April through July, 2,558 leads at $25.31 blended. Spend went from $9K to $32.5K inside a month and CPL moved from $18.74 to $29.85, then recovered to $25.48 and $20.06 over the two months after as creative caught up. Different vertical, but that is usually the shape of scaling. Take the CPL hit, earn it back on creative instead of by pulling spend back down.

We also have a retirement planning practice on Meta right now in the same category, so the compliance side is current work, not something we last touched a year ago.
