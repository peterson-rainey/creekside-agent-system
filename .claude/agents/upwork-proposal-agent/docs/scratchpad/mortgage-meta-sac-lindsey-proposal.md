When Meta rejects one of your ads, is it the ad copy getting flagged or the landing page behind it? That distinction decides whether this is a two week fix or a permanent tax on the account.

The reason I ask is that I ran Meta for a reverse mortgage lender at around $20K a month, and most of the rejections we dealt with did not originate in the ad. They came from the page: a missing disclosure, or a headline promising something the page never qualified. Meta reviews the destination too, and mortgage is one of the categories where it is strict about it.

The other half of this is that Special Ad Category takes away most of the tools people lean on. Age is locked to a broad band, no lookalikes, no exclusions, no detailed demographics, and a 15 mile minimum radius. You cannot target your way to a better lead. Every bit of qualification has to move into the creative, the form, and the event you optimize on. That is where a mortgage account's lead quality problem usually lives, and it is the part that gets missed when someone treats this like a standard Facebook account.

On that account every ad went through a written compliance checklist before it published. Qualifying "no monthly payments" as no monthly mortgage payments, keeping government and agency language out entirely, not running pre approved claims without the conditions attached. Rejections got rare once that was a step instead of a reaction.

Ten years on Meta, plus an ecommerce brand I built and sold. The largest account I personally run right now sits just under $100K a month.

## Screening Q1: Describe your recent experience with similar projects

The closest match is a reverse mortgage lender I ran Meta for from late 2025 until this August, when the engagement ended. It peaked just under $22K in a month, about $149K in Meta spend across the run, all of it inside the Financial Products and Services Special Ad Category.

Two things from that account are relevant to your post.

First, compliance. Every ad went through a written checklist before it published, built off the lender's own requirements and the NRMLA advertising rules. Qualifying "no monthly payments" as no monthly mortgage payments. No government or agency language, no HUD or FHA references, no simulated checks. No pre approved or pre qualified claims unless the conditions were stated with them. Rejections stopped being an event once that was a step in the process instead of a reaction to a disapproval.

Second, on CPL. The cleanest measured stretch was April into May, where cost per lead went from $82 to $59 while volume went up, 109 leads to 140, on slightly lower spend. I will be straight that conversion reporting on that account had gaps across the year, which is why I am quoting the window I can stand behind rather than a blended number for the whole engagement.

Nearest thing to it on volume is a dental group I ran Meta lead forms for into a call center, April through July this year. 2,558 leads at $25.31 blended. The useful part for you is what happened under scale. Spend went from $9K to $32.5K inside a month and CPL went from $18.74 to $29.85. It came back to $25.48, then $20.06, over the two months after, as the creative caught up. That is usually the shape of it. Push volume, take the CPL hit, earn it back on creative instead of by pulling spend back down.

Currently the largest account I run is a cosmetic dentistry and med spa practice at just under $100K a month on Meta, about $1.19 million over the last twelve months. Still active.

Before any of it I built and sold my own ecommerce brand, and I have been on Meta about ten years.
