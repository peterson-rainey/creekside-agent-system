Quick question before anything else: on the Nuts & Bolts site, is the low conversion rate and the trouble customers have placing orders the same problem, or two different ones?

That answer decides where the first dollar goes. If orders are genuinely failing, the conversion rate is not a marketing number, it is a bug report, and traffic spend before that is fixed only buys more people the chance to hit the same wall.

Ten years of my work has been Meta and email for e-commerce brands, plus my own e-commerce business, which I built and sold. The pattern on newly rebuilt Shopify stores is consistent enough that I look for it first. The theme goes live, checkout carries an edge case nobody caught, usually a shipping rule, an app conflict, or a payment method failing on mobile, and the owner reads it as weak demand. Session recordings and a checkout funnel report settle it in a day, and it is the cheapest item on your list.

Carlson is the opposite problem. Demand is proven and production is the cap. Acquisition spend against a supply-constrained brand mostly buys longer lead times and cancellations. Until capacity moves, the honest levers are order value and margin. Bundles, wholesale minimums, and preorder drops that sell against a schedule you control.

On a replacement-parts e-commerce brand I ran on Meta at roughly $13K a month, blended return held at 5.5x across more than 4,500 orders. Different category, same shape as yours. High SKU count, project-driven buyers who already know what they want, and repeat purchase that makes retention worth more than the first order.

Straight with you on fit. Meta, email, and the e-commerce side of Shopify are what I do. Search, local marketing, events, merchandising and affiliate programs are on your list and are not my lane. If what you need is one person owning that entire surface, that is a different hire.

Attached a few results below so you can see how I work an account.

## 1. Examples of measurable growth, with role, duration and numbers

Three, and I run the ad account myself on all of them. Strategy, creative direction and the buying are mine rather than delegated.

**A US replacement-parts e-commerce brand. Ten months.** $131,000 in Meta spend returned $728,000, a 5.5x blended return across just over 4,500 orders. The useful part is the middle. December was the low point of the engagement at 3.22x and $44.93 per order. By April it ran 8.07x at $19.89, on less money, $15,682 down to $13,224, with orders up from 349 to 665. So roughly four months to halve cost per order while nearly doubling volume on a smaller budget.

Some of that was Q4 auction pricing unwinding and I would rather say so. What was mine: I stopped cutting creative on return during Q4, because click-through was rising while return fell, which tells you the creative is fine and the auction is expensive. Then in February I consolidated retargeting from six thin campaigns into one. What makes it checkable is that CPM was flat from January to April, $8.10 to $8.18, so the second half of the gain was not cheaper impressions.

**A regional meal prep brand. Seven months, on $1,000 to $1,500 a month.** Cost per order went from $11.53 in January to $5.50 in July, with monthly orders up from 88 to 260 on a budget that barely moved. August ran back up to $10.71, which is the honest current number rather than the flattering one.

**A cosmetic practice, ongoing.** Just under $100,000 a month in Meta spend, sustained for over a year and still live. That one is a scale and durability example rather than a turnaround.

## 3. Balancing autonomy with owner collaboration, including disagreement

The way I avoid most of it is by agreeing the measure before the work, not after. If we both name what would make a test a success and what number would make me wrong, then disagreement gets settled by the result instead of by whoever feels strongest that week.

Day to day I would rather move and tell you than ask and wait. Budget shifts, creative rotation, audience and campaign structure are decisions I would just make. Anything that touches how the brand sounds, what goes on promotion, pricing, or how you talk to your existing customers is your call and I would bring it to you rather than assume.

When you question a recommendation, my first move is to find out whether it is a data disagreement or a judgment disagreement, because they need different responses. If you know something about your customer that is not in the numbers, you are usually right and I want that early. If it is a read on the same data, I will show you my reasoning and the specific number that would change my mind.

If you overrule me, I run it properly and measure it honestly. What I will not do is keep quietly spending on something I think is wrong, or run a version of your idea that was designed to fail so I can be right about it later.

I would rather be told no in week one than be right in month four.

## 4. Opportunities I already see

Sequenced, because the order matters more than the list.

First, settle whether the low conversion rate and the ordering trouble are the same problem. If orders are genuinely failing, that is a bug rather than a marketing number, and traffic spend before it is fixed only buys more people the chance to hit the same wall. Session recordings and a checkout funnel report answer it in about a day and it is the cheapest thing on the list.

Bundles, wholesale minimums, and preorder drops that sell against a schedule you control. A waitlist on a sold-out precut is a demand asset rather than a lost sale.

Third, retention is probably the most underweighted thing you have. Quilting repeats on a project cadence, so flows, back in stock, and post-purchase sequences earn more per hour than new traffic does at this stage. You have an email expert, so I would coordinate rather than take that over.

Fourth, margin-aware merchandising. Reselling other companies' fabric is thin. Carlson is the owned-margin line. Paid and email should be pushing toward owned brand and high-margin inventory rather than whatever happens to convert.

Fifth, and outside my lane so I flag it rather than claim it, a destination shop has an audience that will travel. Classes, retreats and events are a list-building engine that most online-only competitors cannot copy.
