Q1. Largest ecommerce account across Google Ads, Meta and TikTok, with annual spend and ROAS.

Not one account across all three, and TikTok is the flat part of that answer. Nobody in our book can show you TikTok results, me included.

On ecommerce specifically, mine is a US replacement parts retailer I ran on Meta into May this year. About $131,000 across roughly nine months, 4,500 orders, 5.53x blended, $29 an order on a $161 average order value. Its catalog campaigns carried the account, 10.83x on the first build and 6.96x once we put four times the budget behind them.

For scale rather than category: the largest account I run personally is just under $100,000 a month, about $1.19 million over the past twelve months. It is aesthetics, not ecommerce, and I will not quote you a ROAS on it, because the revenue does not match back cleanly enough for me to stand behind a number.

The Google half of your scope sits with our Google side rather than with me. That book runs about 5.5x on non-branded Shopping and non-branded Search for a Shopify brand.

No single ecommerce account here reaches $500,000 a year on its own. If that is a hard floor rather than a preference, weight this accordingly.

Q2. Have you managed a Google Merchant Center feed directly, not just campaigns?

Directly, no. I have not owned a Merchant Center feed, and I would rather say that plainly than stretch the word managed.

What I do own directly is Meta product catalogs, which is the same discipline one layer across. On the replacement parts account the catalog was the strongest structure in the account, and what made it work was product sets rather than one undifferentiated pool. On another brand I split the feed by availability, so in stock products ran as their own campaign with their own target, separate from cold and bottom of funnel campaigns working the same inventory.

The approach for margin tiers is that mechanic with your attribute. The tier has to exist in the feed as a label before anything can bid on it. Then each tier gets its own campaign and its own target, because a single blended target quietly averages your best and worst products together and buys whatever is cheapest to sell.

Our Google side has run exactly that carve-out on Shopping. All products ran 5.52x, and pulling the best sellers into their own campaign took that subset to 8.30x, same feed, same account, same period.

The tradeoff, and it is the thing I would want agreed before building it: every tier you add splits the conversion data thinner, and bidding on a thin campaign is worse than a blended target on a fat one. Three tiers is usually the ceiling. If your margin bands are narrow, the honest answer may be two tiers plus exclusions.

Q3. First two weeks with no briefing.

Week one I would change almost nothing. The baseline has to come from your sales data rather than the platforms, because whatever I measure against in week one is what I get judged on for the next year. Orders by storefront, by margin tier, by date, reconciled against what each platform claims it drove. That turns your NZ problem into a documented offset instead of a surprise.

Then the inventory: what is live versus paused, which campaigns are budget capped, what the catalogs and product sets look like now, which audiences and exclusions exist, and where conversion events are firing from.

The thing that actually breaks agency handovers is access, not strategy. Pixel history, catalogs, saved audiences and creative libraries sit in assets the outgoing agency may own rather than you, and none of that can be rebuilt afterwards. I would want admin ownership of the business manager, ad accounts, pixels and catalogs confirmed in your name before they walk. Same on the Merchant Center side, which our Google person would run in parallel.

Week two is the first changes, and only where the evidence is unambiguous. Usually consolidation, plus the budget limited campaigns you already flagged, since adding spend to something already efficient is the one change that does not need a new hypothesis first.

Q4. Creative production capability.

An existing team rather than something I would assemble for you. A creatives director and a creative handle static, motion and video. I write the briefs, the angles and the copy, and that split has been in place across every account I have described here.

Specific to wall art, the constraint is not production volume, it is the shot. Most wall art accounts run the artwork flat, straight off the product page, and it performs like a catalog page because that is what it is. Fine in Shopping, where the buyer expects a catalog image. On Meta and Instagram nothing about a flat JPEG tells someone how big the piece is or what the canvas texture does in a room. What moves it is the piece on a real wall at true scale, with enough context that the buyer can size it against their own space.

That is a photography and styling decision more than an editing one, so it is worth agreeing who owns it before the first test round.

Q5. Comfortable working from raw backend sales data, and why necessary.

Yes, and I would push for it even where tracking looks healthy.

It is necessary because platform reported conversions are not wrong at random, they are wrong in a direction. Consent gaps, modelled conversions and cross device journeys all inflate whatever is best positioned to claim credit, which means retargeting and anything optimized to a shallow event.

The replacement parts account is the clearest case I have. It ran separate campaigns optimized to website visits, add to cart, time on site and view content. Every one of them reported a better return than the broad purchase campaign carrying about half the budget and about half the orders. None of them ever held real budget. If you had allocated off platform ROAS you would have moved money toward the campaigns best at taking credit and away from the one actually producing orders.

Three storefronts makes that worse, because you have three attribution surfaces degrading at three different rates. NZ being the weak one is normal rather than exceptional, and the fix is not to repair the tracking before you can act. It is to hold one source of truth, your orders, and treat each platform's number as a signal with a known bias per market.

What I would want from you is orders by SKU or margin tier, by storefront, by date, with an order ID or UTM captured at checkout. On the Google side that same file lets us push corrected values back as offline conversions, which fixes the bidding and not just the reporting.
