# Fit on the Fly - 5 screening answers | Lindsey | 2026-08-30 | UNSENT

## 1. A Meta lead-gen campaign for a B2C brand: offer, spend, CPL

A dental implant practice in Northern California. The offer was a free consultation, delivered through Meta lead forms rather than a landing page, with the leads dialed by an in-house call team. Meta budget was around $20,000 a month. Over a 90 day stretch it produced 2,558 leads at $25.31 each on $64,746 of spend. We ran it split by geography and by language, and the Spanish-language segment came in at $12.99 against $22.79 for English, which is the kind of gap you only find if you build the account to show it to you.

That engagement has ended, so I am describing it in the past tense.

## 2. $750 a month, free six-week program for women over 40, first 30 days

Honest answer first: $750 buys direction, not proof. At $25 a day you will learn which angle people respond to. You will not learn it with statistical confidence, and you will not be able to run a real creative test matrix, because Meta needs roughly 50 conversions per ad set per week to leave the learning phase and every ad set you add splits that budget further.

So the first 30 days would be deliberately narrow:

- Week 0, before any spend. Verify the Pixel fires a clean, deduplicated registration event, and add a server-side event so iOS traffic still reports. If tracking is wrong, everything after it is noise. This is usually a day of work and it is the highest-value thing in the engagement.
- One campaign, one ad set, broad targeting. No interest stacks. At this budget the audience is too small to segment and Meta's delivery does better with room to move.
- Three or four creative angles, not fifteen. Metabolism after 40, the "I have tried everything" angle, a founder-to-camera piece, and one static with the program structure laid out.
- Optimize for the registration event itself. Not landing page views, not clicks. Optimizing on a shallow event teaches Meta to find browsers.
- No retargeting in month one. $750 does not generate enough traffic to build a pool worth retargeting. That is a month two or three decision.

What I would actually recommend is $3,000 a month on Meta once the pilot confirms the offer converts at all. At $750 you should expect to end the month with one working angle and a rough cost per registration, and to treat that as the input for a real test budget rather than as a verdict on the offer.

## 3. Telling high-quality leads from cheap ones

You cannot answer this inside Ads Manager, which is why most reporting on free offers is misleading. Cost per registration is a cost metric, not a quality metric.

For this funnel I would track three things past the signup:

- Open rate on email three, not email one. Email one gets opened by everyone. Email three separates the people who actually intend to do the program.
- Click or reply rate somewhere in the six weeks, because that is the first real signal of intent.
- Eventually, whatever the downstream conversion is. If there is a paid offer after the program, that is the only number that finally settles it.

Then tag every ad with its own UTM so each of those three can be traced back to the specific creative and angle that produced it. That is the part people skip, and without it you are optimizing blind.

The specific risk with a free wellness offer is the freebie audience, people who register for anything free and never open anything. They are cheap, they make the dashboard look excellent, and they are worthless. On the dental account above we solved the equivalent problem by putting qualifying questions on the form on purpose. Cost per lead went up. That was the correct trade, and I would rather have that conversation with you in week two than defend a low CPL in month three.

## 5. A Meta campaign that did not perform initially

A US replacement parts e-commerce brand I ran. Heading into the Q4 holiday period, return on ad spend fell from about 7.7x to 4.3x on flat budget. The obvious read was creative fatigue, and the obvious move was to cut the ads that were dropping.

I looked at the inputs instead. Click-through rate had actually gone up, not down, and CPM had risen about 40%. The creative was not failing. The auction had simply gotten more expensive because every advertiser was bidding through the holidays. Cutting the ads on the blended number would have killed the exact creative that was working hardest.

So we held the winners, stopped judging them on blended return, and re-baselined each angle against its own pre-October numbers. When CPMs normalized in January the account recovered, and by April it was at its strongest return of the entire run at its lowest cost per acquisition.

The lesson transfers directly to a $750 pilot. At small budgets your numbers move for reasons that have nothing to do with your ads, and the most expensive mistake is killing something early because a noisy week looked like a verdict.
