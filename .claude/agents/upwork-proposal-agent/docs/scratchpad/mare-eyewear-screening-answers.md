## Q1 - First hypothesis

The account has never produced enough conversion signal for Meta to optimise with, so what looks like a
creative or audience problem is really an allocation problem. Thirteen purchases in thirty days is about three
a week, against the roughly fifty per ad set per week that purchase optimisation wants. Meta found one pocket
of people that converts and has no data with which to find a second, which is why 59% of spend sits in a single
45-day-old ad. That concentration is the symptom, not the disease. Underneath it the unit economics are tighter
than the ROAS makes them look: at $199 AOV and the 65-75% gross margin typical of acetate frames, break-even
sits around 1.4x, which is exactly where your last thirty days landed. So the account is not slightly
unprofitable, it is at zero, and the distance to real margin is roughly a doubling of ROAS rather than a tweak.
If your true margin is materially different from that assumption, tell me, because it changes the target more
than anything else in the post.

## Q2 - Creative test thresholds

Two numbers. Immediate kill at $150, roughly 1x your CPA, if the creative has produced zero add-to-carts by
then. That one is cheap and unambiguous. Otherwise the verdict comes at $420, roughly 3x CPA, or seven days,
whichever arrives last. Both conditions, not either. Your $68 over five days failed both, which is why it
returned nothing. Seven days is a floor rather than a preference: sunglasses at $199 are a considered purchase
and a meaningful share of orders land two to five days after the first click, so a five-day read systematically
under-counts your best creative. And the verdict is read on cost per add-to-cart, not purchases, because at your
volume a creative fatigues before it accumulates enough purchases to be significant. Worth naming the
consequence: at $2,500 a month that framework affords about six real tests a year. At $5,000 it affords one
every ten days, which is the difference between learning something this quarter and next year.

## Q3 - The 41% initiate-checkout to purchase rate

It is not your problem, and it is the one thing in your post I would push back on. Somewhere between 40% and 60%
is ordinary for DTC checkout completion, so 41% is soft but inside the normal band. Two things make it a poor
place to spend attention. First, the sample is tiny: thirteen purchases against roughly thirty-two initiate-
checkouts means three more orders would read as 50%, so the number is noise more than signal right now. Second,
and this is the real point, the leak upstream is far larger. Working from your own figures, $1,819 at $1.39 CPC
is about 1,300 clicks, 92% of which become landing page views, so roughly 1,200 people reached the site and
about thirty-two of them started a checkout. That is 2.7%. Ninety-seven percent of the traffic you are paying
for never gets near the checkout you are worried about. Fix the product page, the offer and the way lens options
are explained before touching the checkout itself.

## Q5 - Smallest budget taken from unprofitable to profitable

Straight answer, and it is not the one you are hoping for: not at $1,800 a month. The account where I can show
you the clearest structural rebuild was running about $14,000 a month. The levers were cutting the number of
live creatives and consolidating ad sets so the pixel had one place to learn, and it finished around 5.5x
blended across roughly 4,500 orders. Below $5,000 a month I have run plenty of accounts, but I do not have a
clean before-and-after arc to hand you at your budget, and I would rather say that than dress a good week up as
a turnaround. The reason is the same one from my first answer. Under that threshold you cannot accumulate
purchases quickly enough to separate a good decision from a lucky one, so the timeline stretches and the work
gets more judgement-driven, which is exactly the situation you are in now. That is also why I would rather sell
you the audit than a retainer right now.
