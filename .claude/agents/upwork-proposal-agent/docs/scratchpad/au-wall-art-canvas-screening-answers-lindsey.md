Q1. Largest ecommerce account across Google Ads, Meta and TikTok, with annual spend and ROAS.

Not one account across all three, and I would rather say that up front than let it surface on a call. TikTok I have not run, so there is no spend or ROAS for me to give you there. Google sits with a colleague, not with me.

On Meta, the two that are relevant. The one with the cleanest numbers was a US replacement parts retailer, about $131,000 spent over roughly nine months to May this year, around 4,500 orders, 5.53x blended, $29 cost per order on a $161 average order value. The larger one by monthly spend was a DTC home and lifestyle brand I ran from April this year, peaking at $40,389 in July and about $105,000 across the window.

I am quoting the return on the smaller of the two deliberately. On the larger account only about 44% of spend ever had revenue matched back to it, and a blended ROAS built on partial matching is a different number from revenue divided by total spend. The replacement parts account had 85% coverage, so it is the one I will stand behind.

Neither is a $500,000 a year single account on my own. If that is a hard floor, I am not your candidate.

Q2. Have you managed a Google Merchant Center feed directly, and how do you approach feed structure or margin tier bidding?

Google Merchant Center, no. I have never owned one and I have not used Channable.

Meta product catalogs, yes, directly, and that is the part that transfers. On the replacement parts account the catalog work was the strongest structure in the account. The first version ran $3,326 and returned 10.83x on 201 orders. The rebuilt version took $13,789 and returned 6.96x on 595 orders. Both beat the 5.53x account average, and scaling it to roughly four times the budget cost about four points of return. That trade is the whole decision, and you only see it if you are willing to let a campaign's ROAS fall while its order count triples.

On the home and lifestyle account the catalog was segmented by availability rather than sold as one pool. In stock products ran as their own product set off the Shopify feed at $14,644, with a separate cold catalog campaign and a bottom of funnel one working the same inventory at different intent.

That is the same mechanic your margin tiers need: split the catalog into sets, give each set its own campaign and its own target, and stop letting a single blended target average your best and worst products together. Availability is the attribute I have run it on. Margin is a different attribute and the same structure. What I have not done is manage that attribute upstream in the feed itself, which is the Channable half and is genuinely not my skill.

Q3. How would you approach the first two weeks with no briefing?

Week one I would change almost nothing. The first job is a baseline built from your sales data, not the platforms, because whatever I measure against in week one is the number I will be judged on for the next year. That means orders by storefront, by margin tier and by date, reconciled against what each platform claims it drove, so the size and direction of the gap is documented per storefront before anyone touches a budget. Your NZ problem stops being a surprise and becomes a known offset.

Alongside that, an inventory: what is actually live versus what is paused, which campaigns are budget capped, what the catalog and product sets currently look like, what audiences and exclusions exist, and where the conversion events are firing from.

The thing that actually breaks agency handovers is access, not strategy. Pixel history, catalogs, saved audiences and creative libraries live in assets the outgoing agency may own rather than you. I would want admin ownership of the business manager, ad accounts, pixels and catalogs confirmed in your name before they walk, because that history cannot be rebuilt afterwards.

Week two is where the first changes land, and only where the evidence is unambiguous. Usually that is consolidation, plus the budget limited campaigns you already flagged, since those are the one case where you can add spend without needing a new hypothesis first.

Q4. Creative production capability.

I do not produce creative myself. I work with a creatives director and a creative who handle static, motion and video, and I write the briefs, the angles and the copy. That split has been in place across every account I have described here, so it is an existing team rather than something I would go and assemble for you.

Specific to wall art, the constraint is not production volume, it is the shot. Most wall art accounts run the artwork flat, straight off the product page, and it performs like a catalog page because that is what it is. What moves is the piece in a room at scale, so the buyer can size it against their own wall, plus the physical qualities a canvas has and a poster does not. That is a photography and set decision more than an editing one, and it is worth agreeing who owns it before the first test round.

Q5. Are you comfortable working from raw backend sales data instead of GA4 or platform conversions, and why might that be necessary?

Yes, and I would push for it even where tracking looks healthy.

It is necessary because platform reported conversions are not wrong at random, they are wrong in a direction. Consent gaps, modelled conversions and cross device journeys all inflate the campaigns that are best positioned to claim credit, which means retargeting and anything optimized to a shallow event. On the replacement parts account there were separate campaigns optimized to website visits, add to cart, time on site and view content. Every one of them reported a better return than the broad purchase campaign carrying about half the budget and about half the orders. None of them ever held real budget. If you had allocated off platform ROAS you would have moved money toward the campaigns best at taking credit and away from the one actually producing orders.

Three storefronts makes that worse, because you have three attribution surfaces degrading at three different rates. NZ being your weak one is normal rather than exceptional, and the fix is not to repair the tracking before you can act. It is to hold one source of truth, your orders, and treat each platform's number as a signal with a known bias per market.

What I would want from you is orders by SKU or margin tier, by storefront, by date, with something that joins back to the platforms, an order ID or a UTM captured at checkout. With that I can allocate honestly across all three markets even while NZ tracking stays broken.
