**1. Describe your recent experience with similar projects**

Closest match is a cosmetic and aesthetics practice our team runs on both platforms right now. Meta spend there has held between $90K and $115K a month for twelve straight months, and the Google side has run since late April and produced about 550 conversions. Multi account is the normal shape of a week here rather than a stretch, August ran 21 Meta accounts and 16 Google accounts across the book.

The honest edge of that is the product side. We have run a DTC brand selling to women where click through held between four and five percent, but our results sit on the clinic and procedure half of beauty rather than on a shelf of skincare and body care brands. If most of your roster is product rather than treatment, that is worth weighing before you shortlist us.

**2. Please list any certifications related to this project**

Nothing formal to list. No Google or Meta certification badges here, and that is worth saying straight rather than padding the answer.

What sits behind the work instead is volume and policy fluency. Roughly $268K moved across Google and Meta in August alone, and beauty carries rules that decide accounts more often than a badge does. Google's personalized ads health policy blocks remarketing and customer list audiences for cosmetic procedure and body modification services, so a remarketing campaign sitting at low spend with almost no impressions is a policy problem and not a bidding one. Meta's side restricts before and after imagery and implied outcome claims, which is usually what a rejected beauty creative actually tripped. Knowing which of those you are looking at saves a week per incident.

**3. Describe your typical design process and methods**

Worth being clear that we are not the design shop. We write the brief and own the test structure, and someone else makes the asset, so the process below is about deciding what gets made rather than art directing it.

It starts with buyer state and objection, not the brand deck. Most of the actual language gets lifted from reviews and consult call recordings rather than written fresh, because the phrasing a nervous first time patient uses is not the phrasing a copywriter reaches for. From there the brief names four things, the claim, the objection it answers, what has to land in the first three seconds, and what is fixed so the test stays readable.

Then concepts, not variations. Three or four genuinely different reasons to buy running at once, because Meta separates concepts far better than it separates five crops of the same image. Winners earn variations, losers get killed at the concept level rather than tweaked. And for aesthetics the brief has to route around before and after framing from the start, since that is the fastest way to a rejection and a warmed up ad account is not worth risking on it.

**4. Describe how you work on a cross-functional team**

Three handoffs matter and the rest is noise.

With whoever makes the creative, the brief has to name the constraint, not just the vibe. Saying the asset needs to survive a sound off feed and carry the claim in the first three seconds is useful. Saying make it feel premium is not, and it wastes their round.

With the client side, the loop that actually decides lead quality is the front desk. If treatment consults are not being called back inside an hour, paid traffic cannot fix that, and it has to get said plainly even when it is uncomfortable. Same with tracking. Whoever owns the site defines the conversion event, and if that event is a form fill rather than a booked consult, the algorithm optimizes toward the wrong thing all month.

With an account manager or agency lead, one rule. They hear a bad month from us before the client finds it in a dashboard, with the cause and the change already attached.

**5. Where do you get inspiration from?**

Mostly from the client's own customers rather than from other ads. Consult recordings and reviews are where the objections live, and the line that changes performance is usually something a patient already said out loud.

Competitor ad libraries get used, but for longevity rather than for looks. An ad that has been running four months has been paid for repeatedly, which is a signal. An ad that launched last week is somebody guessing. Copying the visual is pointless, copying the angle that survived is not.

On the Google side the search terms report does the same job. It shows the words people actually type, which is often blunter than anything on the brand's site.

Outside of that, mostly out of category. Beauty ads all borrow from each other until every account looks the same, so the useful ideas tend to come from somewhere with different constraints.
