Every launch plan holds until the first dependency slips, and the decision nobody prices in advance is what gets cut. When creative or stock runs late in the final two weeks, does the date hold and something ships thinner, or does the date move? Answer that after the launch has started and it gets answered by whoever is still awake at midnight, differently every time.

Ten years of my work is Meta and email for e-commerce brands. Before that I built and sold my own e-commerce business, so the launch failures I know best are the owner-side ones. Stock landing after the ads did. A page signed off in a doc and never opened on a phone. A flow sent to a list nobody excluded from the ad audience, so you spend to reach people who already bought.

The pattern underneath most missed dates is that the date was never a decision. It was the date the product happened to be ready, worked backwards until it looked like a plan. A date holds when one person owns the dependency list and can cut scope without pulling a founder into the room.

Peak season is worth naming since you mentioned it. On a US replacement-parts brand I ran on Meta, CPM went from $8.06 in October to $11.68 in December, a 45% rise, and cost per order moved $27.50 to $44.93 on the same creative. That is the auction repricing, not the work failing. Recutting creative mid-peak because the dashboard looks bad is the standard mistake, and a launch landing in that window has to expect it.

Straight on fit. Meta, email and the e-commerce side of Shopify are my lane. Snapchat is not, and I would not pretend otherwise about a channel carrying part of your spend.

Attached a few results below so you can see how I work.

Straight answer first. The seat I have held on launches is the operator seat, Meta and email, working inside someone else's launch calendar. I have not carried the launch owner title. Here is the launch work I have actually done, most recent first.

Pre-launch asset checking, this month. A luxury bedding brand, Labor Day promo. Creative was approved and the email templates were built, and the promo mockup carried prices around $2,500 while the store was live at closer to $3,000. Sending that means either honoring a price nobody agreed to or a lot of angry replies. Two more things surfaced in the same pass. The product catalog was returning empty, so the dynamic product blocks had to be rebuilt off event data instead, and the cart button resolved to the raw Shopify subdomain rather than the brand domain. None of that shows up in a creative review. It shows up when someone opens the actual asset against the live store, which is the part of your list I would call the real job.

Three flows shipped as a set, also this month. A home and lifestyle brand moving onto a new brand deck. Welcome, abandoned checkout and abandoned cart, rebuilt together at four, three and three emails, with the exclusion logic mirrored across cart and checkout so the two cannot both fire at the same person. Shipping those one at a time is how brands end up double-sending during launch week.

Peak season, on a US replacement-parts brand I ran on Meta. October to December, CPM went from $8.06 to $11.68, a 45% rise, and cost per order went $27.50 to $44.93. Click-through rose over the same stretch, 1.59% to 1.86%, which is what told me the creative was working and the auction was simply expensive. So the call was to leave the creative alone through the peak. January to April, CPM was flat at $8.10 then $8.18, and cost per order came down to $19.89 on a smaller budget. The flat CPM is what makes that second half checkable rather than just seasonal.

Black Friday at a DTC furniture brand, two years running. 2024 was three campaigns live on the same day, a site-wide sale plus two supporting a separate warehouse sale. 2025 was staged the day before and switched on. Honest read on the outcome: the main sale worked and the follow-on holiday campaign did not, and it got a fraction of the planning the main event did. That is the exact failure a written playbook is meant to stop.

Before agency work I built and sold my own e-commerce business, which is where the stock and margin side of launches comes from.

Where I do not match: no skincare or beauty brand on my list, and Snapchat is not a channel I run.
