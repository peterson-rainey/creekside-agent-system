## 1. Tell us about your experience managing Meta Ads specifically for e-commerce brands.

Straight answer first. My e-commerce work is Meta and email, and the strongest evidence I have is structural rather than a headline number.

The clearest example is a high-AOV replacement parts brand I ran on Meta until it wrapped this spring. It was optimizing on add-to-cart when I picked it up, which is a reasonable-looking setting that quietly teaches Meta to go find browsers. Moving the optimization event onto purchase changed who the algorithm went and found, and that restructure did more for the account than any new creative did.

That account also gave me the cleanest peak-season read I have. Cost per order went from $27.50 in October to $44.93 in December on the same creative, while click-through rose from 1.59% to 1.86% over the same stretch. Rising click-through alongside rising cost is the auction repricing, not the ads failing. From January through April, CPM came back to $8.10 and $8.18 and cost per order came down to $19.89 on a smaller budget. The flat CPM is what makes that second half checkable rather than just seasonal luck.

Right now I run Meta for a luxury sleep brand on Shopify Plus and for a home and lifestyle brand, both DTC, and I own the Klaviyo side on both. Before any agency work I built and sold my own e-commerce business, which is where the margin and stock side of my thinking comes from.

What I do not have is a big-budget scaling story. My e-commerce accounts run roughly $3,000 to $15,000 a month in spend, which I suspect is nearer your situation anyway.

## 3. How do you approach creative testing for a newer brand with a limited advertising budget?

On a small budget the constraint is not creative quality, it is statistics. You cannot run eight cells on a few thousand a month and learn anything from any of them, so the discipline is fewer tests that are actually readable.

Four things I hold to.

Formats go in separate campaigns. If static and video share an ad set, delivery chases the cheaper impression and can starve the format that is actually converting. On the aesthetics account above, video bought impressions at less than half the cost of static and static was still the better buy. Inside a single ad set that finding never surfaces.

One variable at a time. Hook, format and offer changed together tells you the combination moved and nothing about why. On a limited budget that is an expensive thing not to learn.

Test the angle before the polish. Newer brands tend to test five beautiful variations of the same claim. The claim is the variable that moves cost per acquisition. Production value matters, but later.

Fatigue gets measured, not assumed. I work to explicit triggers: frequency crossing about 4.0, or a seven day cost per result running more than 25 percent above the ninety day figure. On one account I left creative running past eight months because frequency was sitting at 1.24 and the seven day number was actually below the ninety day one. Rotating on a calendar instead of on a signal throws away winners and buys a fresh learning phase for nothing, which a small budget cannot absorb.

The other small-budget decision is not to over-segment. Splitting a thin budget across many ad sets keeps all of them stuck in learning. Consolidating is usually worth more than any creative I could add.

## 5. What creative tools do you typically use or recommend for developing Meta ad creatives?

Straight answer, with a limit attached: I am not the person making the asset. I work from brand photography and video, or from what a designer produces.

What I actually work in. Ads Manager for build and delivery. The Meta Ad Library for competitor research, though I use it for longevity rather than aesthetics, since anything running six months or longer is working and that is the only free read you get on somebody else's testing budget. Canva for light iteration, resizing and quick variants when a hook needs testing without going back to a designer for every cut.

Figma I can work from. If your designer builds there I can brief into it and pull what I need, and it is a good place to keep a variant system so that testing a hook does not mean rebuilding the asset each time. I would not represent myself as designing in it.

Kive I have not used, so I am not going to hand you an opinion dressed up as experience. If it is already in your stack, I will learn it.

The more useful answer is that tooling is rarely the constraint. What decides whether a creative test tells you anything is the structure it runs in: formats separated, one variable moving, and enough budget behind each cell to be readable. A better asset inside a bad test structure still teaches you nothing.
