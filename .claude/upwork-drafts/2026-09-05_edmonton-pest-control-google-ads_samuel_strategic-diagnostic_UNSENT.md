Pest control looks like one market and prices like four. Bed bugs and cockroaches are panic buys, searched at 11pm and booked same day, and they carry the most expensive clicks in the category. Ants and wasps are nuisance work with far cheaper clicks and a much longer path to a booked job. Mice run on Edmonton's calendar rather than yours, moving indoors the moment it turns cold. Put all of that in one campaign and the cheap clicks quietly eat the budget while the expensive, profitable calls get starved, and a blended cost per lead hides the whole thing from you.

That is usually the first thing worth changing, and it is why splitting by pest is the right instinct.

1. Yes. Ran Google Ads for a pest control company and produced 79 leads at $92.60 each, cost per lead down 19% and volume up 34% after a restructure. That case study is attached. A mosquito control company in Nashville has also come through our book.

2. Closest example carrying the full picture is a paving and lot striping contractor on Google Search: about $3,870 a month, 199 leads over ten months, $196 per lead, and an 8.86% conversion rate from click to lead. The tighter geographic campaign in that same account ran $131 per lead at 11.07%.

3. Not in Canada. Our local service work is US. Structure, tracking, negatives and auction behavior all carry over cleanly. Edmonton click costs and your real competitor set do not, so those get measured in the audit rather than assumed.

4. CAD $5,000 to $7,000 a month to start. Below roughly $4,000 there is not enough daily budget to run separate campaigns per pest and still feed each one enough weekly conversions to bid on properly. At a $90 to $150 cost per lead, the band our pest and local service accounts actually ran, that range buys roughly 35 to 60 leads a month, which is enough to make search term and negative decisions on data instead of noise. Weight it seasonally rather than flat, and start narrow on geography and the two highest intent pests before widening.

5. $1,500 one time for the audit and rebuild, with the audit included rather than billed separately. Then 20% of ad spend monthly against a $1,500 minimum. At the budget above the minimum is what applies, so $1,500 a month. Fees in USD.

6. Google forwarding numbers on the ads and dynamic number insertion on the site, with a minimum call length set as the conversion so hangups and wrong numbers never count as leads. For booked jobs, capture the GCLID on the form and store it against the job, then import the ones that actually book back as offline conversions so bidding optimizes toward booked work rather than raw calls. That last step depends on whether your booking system can hold and hand back that ID. Worth checking early, because it is the difference between optimizing to calls and optimizing to revenue.

7. Search terms and negatives twice weekly for the first month, then weekly. Bids and pacing weekly. Geography and device monthly, sooner if a surrounding community starts eating budget. Full review and reporting monthly. The recurring work sits with our team rather than with me personally.

8. Conversion tracking, before touching a single keyword. Accounts that spend with little to show are usually measuring wrong rather than buying wrong: duplicate tags, page views counted as conversions, calls counted with no duration floor. One account we took over had duplicate tracking and was effectively only bidding on its own brand name. After that the search terms report, then structure, then whether location targeting is set to presence or to presence and interest, which quietly buys clicks from well outside a service area.
