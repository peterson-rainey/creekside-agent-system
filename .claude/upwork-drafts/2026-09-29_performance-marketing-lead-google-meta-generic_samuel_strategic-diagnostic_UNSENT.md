**What usually breaks a "find more ROI" mandate**

Most Google Ads and Meta accounts get managed against whatever number the platform hands back, ROAS on Meta, conversions on Google, and both of those are the platform's own attribution, not necessarily what actually happened. Blend a few segments or campaigns into one account-level average and you can be quietly scaling a channel that's flat while starving one that's still profitable. That gap, between what the dashboard reports and what's actually incremental, is usually where a scaling decision goes wrong.

**Where we'd start, and where we'd stop**

Before touching bids or creative, we'd want performance broken out by segment, geography, or product line instead of one blended number, and a real read on marginal CAC at the current spend level rather than average CAC. Marginal CAC only means something next to contribution margin, LTV, and payback period though, so the real question is whether the next dollar of spend still clears a profitable payback window, not whether it sits under some fixed CAC target. We'd also want to know how a sale is tracked back to the platforms right now, first-party via CAPI and server-side GA4, or still mostly pixel and click-through, because that decides whether any of those numbers can be trusted in the first place. If the constraint turns out to sit downstream of media, we'd dig into message match between the ad and the landing page, form or checkout friction, and page speed, and come back with specific test hypotheses rather than just tuning bids against a wall. This isn't an audit-and-handoff engagement either, whoever's on this account is building and shipping the changes daily, not writing recommendations for someone else to run.

**Proof, not just process**

I've been running paid ads for six years. Our team runs Google and Meta side by side across several accounts at once right now, and one pattern shows up constantly: on one of our ecommerce accounts, US performance ran close to 3.8x ROAS in the same six-week window a UK segment sat near 0.6x. Blended together, the account read as a flat 2.3x, which hid which half was actually working. On a furniture DTC account we ran, one month that looked like a strong blended return had roughly three quarters of that month's spend sitting inside a single sitewide promo and a retargeting campaign, not new cold-acquisition demand. Separating what's compounding an existing push from what's genuinely new is the habit that keeps a scaling call honest.

A couple things would help scope this right: roughly what you're spending now across the two platforms and how it splits between them, and whether the funnel runs on checkout, a form, or a sales call, since that changes what "incremental" actually means to measure here.
