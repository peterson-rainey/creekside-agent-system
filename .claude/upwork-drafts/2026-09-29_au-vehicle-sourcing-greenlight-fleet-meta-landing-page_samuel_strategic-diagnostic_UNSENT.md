Customers, not leads, is the right frame; it's where high-consideration accounts quietly break. Meta optimises on whatever event you feed it, so a form-submission event just finds more form-fillers, not buyers. It also decides how you retarget someone who visited but didn't enquire: a half-watched walkthrough and a two-second bounce aren't the same audience. Closing that loop is the first thing I'd want solved here, and it's exactly the Conversions API and Pipedrive question you're already asking.

**Case study**

The closest account we've run to this discipline is a Texas land business, not the same model (they hold inventory, you source to a brief) but the same testing principle: every named property got its own campaign and creative test, not one broad campaign averaging everything together. Our team ran Meta there: ~$26,300 over 45 active days, 1,136 leads at a blended $23.19. On one property, the variants with real run time (same audience, same budget) ranged $17.66-$32.99 CPL by creative alone; a fifth, newer variant was too early to read and sits outside that range. Reporting stopped at lead, not sale, so by design we optimised for lead volume. That's the gap your Pipedrive and Conversions API setup closes: without a lead-to-customer signal, cost per lead looks fine while cost per customer quietly worsens as spend scales.

**Screening questions**

1. Opening line: your ute is costing you jobs before it's even broken down. Headline: we handle the quotes, the trade-in and the paperwork, you handle the job. A tradie reads that line as his own numbers, not a pitch, because it names a cost he already carries, not a vehicle spec, separating someone weighing a replacement from someone browsing. The landing page needs to open on that same cost-of-waiting line, not a generic get-a-quote headline, or the click reads like bait and switch.

2. First, mismatch: the ad promises speed, the form asks generic details, so fast-answer seekers bail. Check form completion against click-through and read enquiry text for vagueness. Second, response speed: high-consideration buyers shop while intent is hot, and slow first contact kills conversion. Check time-to-first-contact against sale rate. Third, targeting pulls researchers months from buying. Check lead-to-appointment rate by audience, and ask sales whether no-outcomes were timing or fit.

3. Two genuinely different concepts: a tradie on camera on why he stopped buying straight from a dealer, versus a founder-led explainer on how sourcing and negotiation work. Different hooks, different proof. A refinement: testing that same tradie concept with three opening lines on the same footage. With modest budgets, I wouldn't call a concept until it's run long enough for a stable cost per lead across a week, not a burst of cheap early clicks.

4. The decision: splitting one campaign into one per property, each testing several creative formats against the same offer, instead of letting Meta split the budget on its own. Creative caused the difference because audience and budget held constant on the same property, only creative changed, and CPL still ranged $17.66-$32.99 across the variants with real run time. That isolates the variable in a way an account-level before-and-after can't.

5. Andromeda is the retrieval stage: it narrows tens of millions of ad candidates to a relevant few thousand before ranking picks what's shown. Its real unlock is handling far more creative variety there without blowing up cost, which is why it matters least for Greenlight now: a handful of concepts won't feed that advantage yet. Worth building toward, not optimising for day one. Separately, a rule I disagree with: let the algorithm find your audience, don't restrict targeting. True at volume, false early. A few conversions a week won't give Meta enough signal to exit learning cleanly either way, so broad targeting on a shallow event just teaches it to find more of the same, cheaply.
