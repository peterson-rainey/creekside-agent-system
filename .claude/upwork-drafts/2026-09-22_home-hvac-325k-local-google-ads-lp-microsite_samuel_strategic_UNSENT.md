Home HVAC leads mostly arrive as phone calls, and that should shape how a small Google test gets built. A call placed from the ad never reaches the website, so if the call button shows the office's own number, those calls can't later be matched to booked jobs and sent back to Google. Using Google's forwarding number from day one means the test gets judged on booked jobs, not clicks. Location needs the same care: Google's default also reaches people outside your area who just search your city, so we target people in the service area.

The second decision is which service goes first. A small budget split across repair, replacement, maintenance and ductwork leaves each one too few calls to read, and Google says a new bidding strategy needs up to around 50 conversions or three conversion cycles to learn. With heating season coming, furnace work should lead. Repair books on the phone and produces calls fast, at a small ticket. Replacement is the bigger job with a longer decision, so each lead costs more and takes longer to judge. Which one pays better for you decides where the first dollars go.

Local Services Ads show above the regular ads and charge per lead, but they need license and insurance verification first, so that paperwork starts in week one.

On pages, I'd hold off on a microsite until the ads show which service earns its own page. For now, a "furnace repair" click should land on a furnace repair page, with the number tappable at the top on a phone.

Straight with you: our team hasn't run an HVAC account. Our trades work on Google is paving, construction and home services. For a Virginia paving company, a Search campaign and a Performance Max campaign ran over the same four months at nearly the same spend, and Search came in at $131 per lead against $268. A Tennessee home builder went from 15 to 60 leads a month, with cost per lead down from $454 to $239. For a Tennessee home services company, Local Services Ads brought in 230+ phone leads and 70+ message leads over twelve months.
