An established business with Meta already running almost never has a media buying problem. It has a signal problem, and in our experience it takes one of three shapes. Thirty minutes inside the account tells you which one you have.

Shape one: the account is optimizing to something that is not the money event. Purchase or booked consultation gets swapped for add to cart, landing page view, or a click objective, usually because the volume column looked healthier that way. Meta then goes and buys the cheapest possible version of a person who does that shallow thing. The dashboard improves and the bank account does not.

Shape two: the budget is spread across more ad sets than the account can support. Meta needs roughly 50 conversions per ad set per week to exit the learning phase. An account running eight ad sets on forty weekly conversions has zero ad sets that ever learn, so every one of them is permanently guessing. Consolidation is the fix, and it feels like giving up control right up until the cost per acquisition drops.

Shape three: prospecting and retargeting are bidding against each other. Warm audiences never got excluded from the cold campaigns, so the account pays a premium to reach people it already owned. This one is invisible in the campaign view and obvious the moment you look at audience overlap.

All three are checkable before anyone touches a budget, which is how we would start. Week one is a full account audit: event and Conversions API integrity first, then structure and learning phase status, then audience overlap, then creative fatigue by frequency and first-impression ratio. You get the findings in writing whether or not the diagnosis is flattering to whoever built the account.

From there the work is ordinary and it is the part most accounts skip. Rebuild around the real conversion event, consolidate until ad sets can actually learn, separate cold from warm cleanly, and run creative against a proper testing structure rather than swapping images and hoping.

What backs this up. A coaching and education client came to us with a fragmented Meta account. Restructuring it took cost per acquisition from $256 to $123 and monthly applications from 41 to 90, on five percent more spend. Same budget, different architecture. That account is still with us and still running.

On scale, we manage roughly $185,000 to $230,000 a month in Meta spend across our client book, with single accounts up to about $99,000 a month, so the structure above is not theory from a small account.

One thing worth pricing in now if your business has a seasonal peak. Across our entire Meta book last year, cost per thousand impressions went from $22.65 in September to $33.90 in December on roughly flat spend. Auction costs climb about fifty percent heading into Q4 regardless of how good the account is. Budgets that ignore that get read as a performance failure in December when they are really a planning failure from September.

Three things I would need to size this properly, and I would rather ask than assume:

What is the current monthly Meta spend, and what can a customer cost for the math to work? A cost per acquisition target without a customer value behind it is just a number somebody liked.

What event does the account optimize to today, and does the pixel or Conversions API fire on the actual sale or booking? This single answer usually predicts most of the first ninety days.

Where do the ads run, and how long has the account been active? A five year old account carries history worth keeping. A restarted one does not, and the plans are different.
