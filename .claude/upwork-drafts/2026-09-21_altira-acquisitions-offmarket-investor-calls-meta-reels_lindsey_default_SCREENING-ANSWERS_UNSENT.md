**1. Describe your recent experience with similar projects**

Everything close to this in my book is a high-ticket decision people make after a conversation, not at a checkout.

The nearest in shape is the implant practice from my cover letter, which had two offices and a call center working the leads from my Meta forms. Leads nobody has time to call don't book, so in June I cut daily spend by more than half to match what the call center could handle.

For a lender selling reverse mortgages to older homeowners, my Meta spend peaked just under $22,000 in a month. My largest account right now is a cosmetic dental practice spending close to $100,000 a month on Meta.

I haven't run a real estate investor account yet. What carries over is sorting people by the size of the decision before the call, the way your quiz already sorts them by capital, and making sure the numbers count calls, not form fills.

**2. How do you use metrics to inform your strategy?**

Mostly by splitting the numbers until an average can't hide anything, then judging each piece on the step that turns into revenue.

A recent one: a dental practice I run had two campaigns going in August, one on an engagement objective and one on leads. The engagement campaign spent $1,845.88 and got nearly twice the impressions, with not one recorded enquiry. The leads campaign spent $1,955.17 and brought in 124 leads at $15.77. Blended, that reads as $30.65 a lead, a number that describes neither campaign.

I also check that a metric measures what it says. That's why the Calendly event in my cover letter comes first: until a finished booking reaches Meta, cost per booked call is really cost per opened calendar.

Once it does, your Reels get judged the same way. A Reel with great organic views isn't automatically a good ad, so each one runs on a leads objective and earns more budget by producing booked calls and $75K-plus applicants. And before the test starts, I'd agree with you on the one number that decides it.
