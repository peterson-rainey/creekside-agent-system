1. Tell me about a Meta campaign you personally managed. Who were you trying to reach, what did you test, and what were the results?

The clearest one is a luxury furniture brand where I ran Meta. We were reaching people furnishing a home with high-end pieces, a slow and considered purchase, so cold prospecting carried the account. Instead of one broad prospecting campaign, I ran prospecting by product so each one could be judged on its own. Over the same stretch, going by Meta's attributed revenue, the strongest product returned about $2.36 for every dollar spent, the second about $1.36, and the general campaign under 10 cents. A chair campaign returned 40 cents and I shut it off after five days of spend. Budget then moved to the products earning it. It wasn't a controlled test, since the products sit at different prices, but it gave a fast keep or kill read on each one. I'd run your course and mentorship the same way, with each pain point in its own lane.

2. If the ad brings in the wrong people, how do you decide whether to change the targeting or the creative?

I look at where the wrong people are coming from before changing anything. If they show up across every audience and placement, the ad itself is attracting them, so it's a creative problem, and new targeting just finds more of the same people. If they cluster in one ad set, age band or placement, that's targeting. With how broad Meta's targeting runs now, the creative does most of the filtering anyway: the hook and the promise decide who stops scrolling. For your mentorship, I'd read the applications against the ad each one came from. If one hook keeps bringing in people looking for a quick way into acting rather than serious training, that's the ad I'd rework first, and I'd keep the audience that's already finding your best students.
