Hi there,

In high-value service accounts, a lot of the wasted spend hides in the conversion setup, not just the keywords. Google counts a form fill from someone looking for free tax software the same as one from a business owner who needs an accountant, and Smart Bidding will tend to chase whichever is cheaper to get. Tying signed clients and revenue back to the campaigns fixes the reporting, and once there are enough of them, it also tells Google which leads to buy more of.

**What our team would check first in the audit**

1. Conversion actions. Which ones are primary, whether any are counted twice (a native Google Ads form conversion plus the same form imported from GA4 is a common one), and whether calls have a minimum length so hang-ups don't count as leads.
2. Search terms and settings. In tax and accounting the common leaks are software and DIY searches ("free," "login," "template"), job seekers ("salary," "jobs," "CPA exam") and people looking for the IRS itself. Those go into negative lists on day one, along with location set to people in your area rather than people interested in it, and Search Partners and auto-applied recommendations reviewed.
3. Structure and match types. Branded searches get their own campaign so they don't make new-client cost look cheaper than it is, and each service line gets its own campaign so budget follows the one that's worth the most. Service terms start on exact and phrase. Broad match comes in only once conversion data is clean, and ad copy is tested one angle at a time (fixed fee, credentials, response time).
4. Quality Score. We use its three parts (expected CTR, ad relevance, landing page experience) to decide which keyword groups need new copy and which need a better page. Google's own help calls it a diagnostic, not an input to the auction, so the parts matter more than the score.

**Pages, tracking and reporting**

For a professional firm, the page has to show who will handle the work, their credentials, what happens after the form (a call within one business day, for example) and a phone number that works on mobile, before any design polish. Our tracking specialist sets up and checks GA4, Google Tag Manager and the Google Ads tag, then submits test leads through every path to confirm each one fires once.

Qualified leads go back into Google Ads first, with signed clients and revenue added as they build up. Google won't import a conversion uploaded more than 90 days after the ad click, so on a longer sales cycle we'd send an earlier stage your team trusts, like a booked consultation. You'd have a live dashboard you can check any day, plus a written breakdown every two weeks of spend, leads, qualified leads, clients and revenue by campaign. Scaling comes once cost per signed client holds steady, with budget raised in steps on the campaigns producing clients and bid strategy changes made one campaign at a time, since each one puts the strategy back into learning. A senior Google Ads specialist on our team runs the account day to day.

If individual tax is part of your mix, timing matters: the extension deadline is October 15, so that demand peaks now and drops right after. We'd judge cost per lead on a longer window than the last few weeks and have tracking settled before the January ramp.

**Relevant work**

We haven't run campaigns for an accounting or tax firm. The closest is professional-services lead gen for a bankruptcy law firm in Orange County that was running everything in one campaign. We restructured it into market-specific campaigns. Compared with the prior period, conversions went from 117 to 229 and cost per conversion from $86.09 to $50.29, on about $1.4K more spend.
