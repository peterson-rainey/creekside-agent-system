## ANSWERS

**1. Please share one successful Google or Meta ad campaign you personally managed. What was the goal, approximate ad spend, what did you personally handle, and what measurable results did the campaign produce?**

A paving and sealcoating contractor in Virginia, on Google. The goal is paving leads at a cost the jobs can carry, in a trade where search clicks in this account run about $14 to $18.

Spend is about $8,000 a month, roughly $80,900 over the past year. Our Google specialist runs the account day to day. I set the strategy and review the numbers with them.

The decision that mattered most was a head-to-head. A Search campaign and a Performance Max campaign ran over the same four months at nearly the same spend, about $5,750 and $5,640. Performance Max bought clicks at $3.00 against $14.53, so on a click report it looked like the better buy. On leads it was the reverse: Search brought 44 at $131 each, Performance Max 21 at $268. Budget went where the leads were.

Over the past year the account produced about 391 leads at roughly $207 each. The last 30 days ran $7,011 for 42 leads, about $167 each, so the cost is still coming down as the account matures.

**2. How do you approach difficult conversations with customers?**

By deciding what's ours to answer before the first hard comment shows up. In week one I'd agree a short list with you. The first group we answer directly: schedules, format, how to enroll. The second goes to a private message. The third comes straight to you: refunds, a dog with a bite history, anything about a specific dog's behavior or health, and anything the program itself would have to promise.

On a hard one, the reply is short and gives one next step, such as moving it to a message with a named time for a response, and it goes out the same business day. Waiting is what turns a question into a complaint.

Dog training tends to have a comment problem most categories don't: people arguing about training methods under the ad. We wouldn't debate methodology in the comments. One calm line pointing to how you train, then leave it. A long back-and-forth under an ad reads worse for the brand than the original comment.

The same rule applies to conversations with you. If a test doesn't work, you hear it early, with the number and what we're changing, not at the end of the month.

**3. How do you use metrics to inform your strategy?**

First by agreeing which number the account is allowed to chase. For a premium program that's enrollments, or booked consultations if a call comes first, not clicks or cheap leads. Everything else is diagnostic: click-through rate and CPM tell us why something moved, not whether it worked.

Second, by reading numbers at the right time. Google reports conversions on the click date, so the newest weeks always look worse than they'll finish. A Meta ad set needs roughly 50 results a week before its numbers settle. Changing things faster than that just resets the test, so we change one thing at a time and give it a fixed window.

Third, by letting the data pick the next test instead of opinion. The paving account above is the example: cheaper clicks looked like the answer until leads were counted. For you, the same search-term data that steers budget also shows which topics convert, which feeds straight into what you write next.
