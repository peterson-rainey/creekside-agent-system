A Meta account can be managed well and still not pay, and when that happens it is rarely the auction. It is usually the campaign objective, which gets set once during setup and then quietly governs what the algorithm spends the budget buying. That is the first thing I look at, because it is the cheapest thing to check.

Here is what that costs, with real numbers off our own book. One account ran two campaigns side by side last August. Same account, same audience pool, same launch day, one set to Engagement and one set to Leads. The Engagement campaign spent $1,845.88 and bought 49,760 impressions and 1,584 clicks, nearly twice the audience of the other one. It recorded not one enquiry. The Leads campaign spent $1,955.17 on roughly half the impressions and produced 124 enquiries at $15.77 each. The account rollup reported $30.65 per enquiry across both, a figure that described neither campaign. Reading that rollup alone you would conclude enquiries cost twice what they actually cost, and you would keep funding the half that was only buying an audience.

One limit I will state rather than let you find it. An engagement objective would not record lead events anyway, and that account had no call tracking, so I cannot prove it drove zero phone calls. That is the point. The measurement gap and the performance gap are usually the same gap.

**Why the setting survives so long**

Because the cheap outcome always looks like progress. Ask Meta for a cheap interaction and it will reliably find people who interact cheaply and never buy. The report improves while the bank account does not. Across roughly 1,825 Meta campaigns on our book, fewer than one in ten runs on an engagement or video objective, and that is a consequence of the above rather than a stylistic preference.

**The other thing that keeps an account guessing**

Meta needs somewhere around 50 results per ad set per week to leave the learning phase. An account spread across eight ad sets on forty weekly conversions has no ad set that ever learns, so all of them are permanently estimating. Consolidation is the fix, and it feels like losing control right up until acquisition cost drops. Worth knowing before either of us touches anything: adding an ad or changing the bid strategy restarts learning, so a habit of daily adjustments can hold an account in that state indefinitely. Data driven and frequently driven are not the same discipline.

**What the first two weeks look like**

Pull ninety days and split every campaign three ways. By objective, by audience temperature, and by new customer against returning. Then check pixel and Conversions API integrity against the event that actually makes you money, then audience overlap, then creative fatigue by frequency. You get the findings in writing whether or not they flatter whoever built the account. Nothing gets rebuilt in week one.

**Evidence the rebuild half works**

A US replacement parts ecommerce brand came to us on roughly $15,000 a month. In the month we inherited it, cost per purchase was $34.72. By April it was $19.89 on slightly less spend, and the cost to reach a thousand people barely moved across that window, $8.10 against $8.18, so the change sat in structure rather than in a cheaper auction. May settled at $22.91. That account has since left us, so past tense on it. I will not attach a single mechanism to the drop, because the audit changed targeting and creative inside the same window and the honest answer is that several things moved at once.

**One gap, stated up front**

We hold no platform certifications. I raise it because posts like yours often screen for them and you should hear that from me rather than find it later.

Day to day the account would sit with a dedicated Meta buyer on our side rather than being handed down to someone junior after signing, sized to a lighter weekly commitment rather than a full-time seat.

**Four questions**

What is monthly Meta spend today, and is this a conversation closer to five thousand a month or closer to twenty five?

What conversion event is each campaign optimizing toward, and does that event fire on an actual sale or booking?

Are you tracking what a genuinely new customer costs, separately from the blended number?

What does a customer need to cost for this to be worth running?

Answer those and I can tell you which of the two problems above you are actually dealing with, before either of us spends a dollar finding out.
