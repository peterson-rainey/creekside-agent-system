## PROPOSAL

On a low-volume industrial account, the number most likely to mislead you isn't the CPC, it's the conversion count. When a month only produces a couple dozen form fills, a few support requests, job seekers or vendors inside that count are enough to teach automated bidding to go find more of them, and at industrial CPCs every one of those lessons is expensive. That's why the first thing I'd settle is what the account counts as a lead, since every other finding reads differently after that.

That was the headline finding on an industrial equipment manufacturer's Google Ads account our team audited last month: support requests landed in the same conversion as quote requests, so the reported cost per lead looked better than the real one.

Three other things behave differently at low volume. Broad match on thin industrial terms drifts toward nearby searches that have volume, like job hunting, how-to questions and consumer versions of the product. The search terms report, read against what actually turned into sales conversations, catches that faster than the keyword list. Target CPA on a handful of conversions a month tends to throttle delivery, which shows up as impression share lost to rank and often gets blamed on competitors outbidding you. And Google's default location setting reaches people outside your territory who have only shown interest in it.

The closest account our team manages is a commercial paving and striping company that sells to property managers and general contractors, where the main Search campaign averages over $17 a click. In one market, on near-identical spend over the same months, Search delivered leads at $131 and Performance Max at $289, even though PMax clicks were about five times cheaper. Cheap clicks and cheap leads are different things. On B2B the number that should move budget is what a qualified lead costs, so the review needs your sales team's read on which leads were real.
