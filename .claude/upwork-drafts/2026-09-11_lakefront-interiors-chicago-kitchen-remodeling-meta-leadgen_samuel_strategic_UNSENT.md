A held in-home estimate for a kitchen remodel usually lands a week or more after the lead. Someone fills out the form on a Tuesday, gets booked for the following weekend, then shows or cancels after that. On a 10 to 14 day pilot, most of the good leads will be on the calendar but not yet held when the test ends, so cost per held estimate will read worse than it really is.

That timing also shapes the Meta setup. Meta optimizes toward whatever event you send it. If that event is a plain form submit, it finds people who like submitting forms, which is where renters and faucet-swap quotes come from. Meta can't reliably tell you who owns their home, so the form has to. It should ask the questions your sales team actually screens on and only count a lead as qualified when the answers pass. A tougher form costs more per lead and cuts the junk. Once booked and held estimates are logged, those get sent back to Meta so it learns from the people who actually let you in the house. That takes volume, so it's a month-two step, not a week-one step.

For Chicago, a zip list built around single-family neighborhoods usually beats one radius from downtown, since a radius pulls in a lot of condo and rental traffic. Creative should show real finished kitchens and give a clear reason to book the estimate rather than lead with a discount.

To be straight on fit, our team runs Meta lead gen for local service and high-ticket consultation businesses, but our documented home services results are mostly on Google, so I won't hand you a Meta remodeling number we don't have.

On the $150 to $200 target, the management fee is a fixed floor at lower spend, so the all-in cost per estimate improves as volume grows. The 90 days is mostly the lag above: two weeks won't show what a held estimate really costs you.
