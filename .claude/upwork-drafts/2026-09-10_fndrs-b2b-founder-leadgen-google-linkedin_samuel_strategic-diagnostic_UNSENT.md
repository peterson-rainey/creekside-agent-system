One number first. Two Meta accounts, one owner, financial advisory, same lead objective, June 1 to September 8. One paid $175.55 CPM and returned leads at $182. The other paid $28.97 CPM and returned leads at $1,906. Six times the media price, a tenth of the lead cost, and the weak account held 43% of the budget. Every top-of-funnel metric said the wrong account was winning.

1. Most relevant campaign. The account above: event registration into a private appointment, running now, $32,950 producing 181 registrations at $182. Older affluent audience, high consideration, though B2C not B2B. My closest true B2B engagement ran Meta for category awareness and Google for the demand it created, the same diversification you describe. It churned without an acquisition-cost number, so I will not put a figure beside it.

2. Google Search and LinkedIn together. We have not run LinkedIn Ads. No accounts, no spend, no results, and I will not offer to bring in someone who has, because we do not have them. No platform certifications either. Google Search is the depth, 16 accounts active today. What I can tell you about LinkedIn is structural: it is the only channel where owning a business is declared by the member rather than inferred, which is why it fits this audience and why it prices as it does.

3. Testing boomers without restricting to them. Age on Search is observed, not chosen by the searcher, and a real share of impressions land in Undetermined. Restrict to 55+ and you throw that bucket away, buyers included. Leave targeting open, carry age as a reporting dimension from day one, and read it at the qualified stage rather than on cost per lead. The qualified rate is where a segment shows up or does not. Bid adjustments once a band clears noise, dedicated campaigns later. Splitting early is what stalls accounts.

4. Other audiences. The variable is transition stage, not birth year, so I would test it in the offer, not the targeting. A valuation or readiness assessment pulls the owner with no roadmap. An owner-dependency angle pulls the profitable-but-trapped operator. Build-value-now-decide-later pulls the founder with no exit date, the largest and least competitive segment. Run them as message tests against an open audience and let age fall out of who self-selects.

5. GoHighLevel. We run our own GHL instance, so this is hands-on. UTMs plus the click ID written to a contact custom field via GTM and a server-side endpoint, so attribution survives the form. Trigger on pipeline stage changes, not tags, because stage is what a sales team actually maintains. Worth knowing: workflow logic is readable but not authorable through the GHL API, so sequences get built in the UI regardless of who does it. The gap, straight: the capture leg is built, on our own funnel, no client performance attached. Pushing closed-won values back as offline conversions is specced and not shipped.

6. First 30 days. Tracking before spend. Validate GA4 and GTM, define the conversion set, confirm the CRM writes back, standardise naming and UTMs. Then Google Search only, tightest intent you have, open on demographics. LinkedIn launches once Search has produced a qualified-cost number to measure it against.

7. Raw lead versus qualified opportunity. That definition is your sales team's to write, and it has to exist as a pipeline stage before launch, not an opinion afterwards. Ask them for the two or three disqualifiers that come up most on calls and encode those. The part most setups miss is the return trip. A qualified stage that lives only in the CRM improves reporting. One that goes back to the platform changes what the platform buys. Until that loop closes you are optimising to form fills whatever the dashboard says.

Prior-period comparison on every line, an explicit scale, keep and kill call with reasoning, and a data-sources-and-limits block naming what is not connected yet. Given tracking accuracy is on your list, read that block.
