Before anyone blames the 17 August bidding change, split the drop in two. If clicks and spend held steady but recorded purchases fell, the bidding change is a bystander. The algorithm is starving on missing conversion data. If clicks fell first, it is bidding or budget.

Squarespace makes the first case likely. Afterpay and Link send buyers off-site to pay. When they come back, the purchase tag often fires twice, fires without value or transaction ID, or never fires at all. A tROAS campaign fed half its orders will cut bids to hit a target it thinks it is missing. That looks exactly like an algorithm update.

HOW WE INVESTIGATE THE DECLINE

1. Reconcile 60 days of Squarespace orders, split by card, Link and Afterpay, against Google Ads and GA4 purchases by transaction ID. This shows the gap per payment method, including duplicates.
2. Compare 28 days before and after 17 August for each campaign: clicks, CPC, impression share lost to budget versus rank, conversions and value. Then line those up against change history.
3. Check which campaigns showed "limited by budget" on 17 August. If only those declined, the update is a real factor. If everything declined, it is not.
4. Check Merchant Center diagnostics for the same dates. Adult classification, disapprovals and product ID changes can remove products from auctions without any alert inside Google Ads.

You get written findings and a ranked change list. Nothing gets rebuilt until you approve it.

MARGIN, NOT REVENUE

Your math is right. 400% is break-even and 600% leaves about $0.50 per dollar. We would bid on margin bands, not one account-wide target. We tag each product by contribution margin using a custom label in a supplemental feed. Each band gets its own tROAS, calculated from its own margin. Thin-margin wines either get a higher target or are excluded, and you approve each exclusion. Brand stays in its own campaign, so non-brand gets measured on its own. New customers are reported separately from returning ones.

Fees are part of that equation. A percentage fee at our top rate takes $0.20 of each spend dollar. At 600%, that leaves you about $0.30. If your spend is small enough that our minimum pushes that below zero, we will tell you at the audit stage rather than sell you Stage 2.

Home goods ecommerce on Google, an account we built and ran: over the same December to July window, Performance Max returned 5.29x and standard Shopping returned 5.52x. PMax lost, so we turned it off. In that account, one best-selling product line carved out of the non-brand Shopping pool returned 8.30x, against 5.47x for the pool it came from. We also have a counter-example in the same account where segmenting PMax did worse. That is why we treat structure as something the audit tests, not an assumption.

Luxury mattress brand, Merchant Center: automatic discounts on the store were not syncing to Google as sale prices. Shopping ads showed prices about $599 above the website. We fixed it with sale price overrides, killed a duplicate feed, and moved micro-events that were inflating conversion value to secondary. That is the same class of problem as your transaction ID and duplication checks.
