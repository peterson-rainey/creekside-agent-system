## PROPOSAL (paste as-is)

The thing most likely to cost you money here is not the creative pipeline. It is the calendar.

Across the Meta accounts in our book, twenty-one to twenty-three accounts spending around $200,000 a month between them, cost per thousand impressions ran $24.45 in October, then $33.78 in November and $33.90 in December, then fell back to $24.17 in January. Roughly 38 percent more expensive to reach the same person, for eight weeks, then it lets go. That is not one account having a bad quarter. That is the auction.

An October 4 drop sits on the last cheap weekend of the year. A brand new ad account whose pixel has never fired a purchase will spend that weekend in learning, which means the account does its most expensive learning in its most expensive month.

That changes what late September is for. It is not account hygiene. It is buying cheap conversion signal on existing catalog product so the pixel has something to optimize against when drop traffic hits, and so the retargeting pools are already populated the day the drop opens.

Second thing, and this is the one that usually breaks the timeline.

Spark Ads and Meta partnership ads are not a creative format. They are a permissions format. A Spark Ad runs on the creator's own post through a spark code only that creator can generate from their own account, and a Meta partnership ad needs each creator to authorize it from their own profile. A fan video that arrives as a file is not an ad yet. Download it and reupload it and it runs as a brand ad: the handle goes, the organic view count goes, the comment thread goes, and the social proof that made the video worth buying goes with them.

So if customer videos are coming in from a promo, the authorization has to be collected inside the promo, at the moment someone submits, not chased afterward. Chasing it afterward is where these launches lose two weeks.

Third, and this one is about the November drop more than the October one. An audience that size makes week one look free. Warm traffic off a following that large returns a cost per purchase that will not survive contact with cold audiences. One account in our book, direct to consumer at a considered price point, spent $105,010.24 on Meta across four months, scaling from $12,373.92 in the first month to $40,389.21 in the fourth. CPM over that same stretch went from $34.94 to $78.88. Nothing broke. That is what buying more of an audience costs. Whatever October reports, the November plan gets built off the cold number.

On your requirements, straight answers in both directions.

Meta at that spend level in direct to consumer ecommerce, yes. The account above averaged around $26,000 a month and peaked past $40,000, Shopify, sold to consumers.

TikTok, no. There is no paid TikTok account in our book, not at that budget and not at any number. Meta, Google, Local Services, programmatic and email all show up across the roster. TikTok does not appear once. If managed TikTok spend in that band is a hard requirement, someone else clears it and you should know that before you read further.

Spark Ads and partnership ads with results I can point at, also no. Of more than 1,800 Meta campaigns in the book, two were creator sourced. One never spent. The other ran $3,930.66 with no recorded purchases. What I bring there is the mechanism and the sequencing, not a scorecard.

So the honest shape of it. The Meta build, the tracking from day one, and the drop sequencing are work I would take and can defend line by line. The TikTok side would be first campaigns at that spend for our team, and it should be scoped and priced as that rather than dressed up as something else.

Two things before any of this becomes a plan. Is the $20,000 a month an ongoing monthly figure or is it concentrated into drop weeks, and is that a ceiling or a starting point? And when you grade this in November, are you grading on revenue from the drop, on sell through of the units you produced, or on the size of the retargeting audience you own going into the next one?
