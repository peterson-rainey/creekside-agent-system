Setting up a Google Ads account is about four hours of work. Deciding what the account is allowed to count as a win is the part that determines whether it makes money, and it is almost always settled before launch by someone who was not thinking about it. Three decisions do most of the damage.

The first is what counts as a conversion. Smart bidding is obedient rather than intelligent. It buys more of whatever you tell it a conversion is, so if the account counts every form fill, every phone tap, and every newsletter signup, Google will go find you the cheapest possible version of a person who does those things. The dashboard fills up and the sales pipeline does not. The fix is to name the money event before launch, and if the sale actually closes on a call or after a quote, to feed the closed deals back into Google rather than letting it optimize to the top of the funnel forever.

The second is brand and non-brand running in the same campaign. People searching your company name were already coming. They convert cheaply and they drag the blended number down until it hides everything underneath it. Real figures from one account we run, over the last twelve months: branded search returned a $16.90 cost per conversion at $0.36 a click, while unbranded search in that same account returned $104.25 at $1.26. Same business, same period, roughly the same spend on each. Blend those two together and you get a comfortable average that tells you nothing about whether you can acquire a new customer profitably, which is the only question that matters.

The third is what happens in the first thirty days of a brand new account. A new account has no conversion history, which means smart bidding has nothing to bid from. Broad match plus a target cost per acquisition on day one spends the entire learning budget discovering that your product is adjacent to a dozen searches you would never have paid for. Starting tighter than feels comfortable, reading the search terms report daily rather than monthly, and expanding only where there is evidence gets to the same place with a lot less tuition.

How I would run it. Week one is the tracking build and the account architecture: conversion actions defined and tested end to end, brand separated from non brand from the start, geography and budget set against a cost per customer you can actually afford. Then launch narrow. Then the first three to four weeks are search term mining and negative keyword work, which is unglamorous and is where most of the early savings live. Structural changes and bid strategy shifts come after there is enough conversion data to justify them, not before.

For scale, we currently manage roughly $90,000 to $140,000 a month in Google spend across 13 to 16 active accounts, in search, shopping, and Performance Max. That is a real book rather than a single account, so the patterns above come from repetition.

Three things I would need to size this properly, and I would rather ask than guess:

What is the monthly budget you have in mind, and what can a new customer cost before the math stops working? A cost per acquisition target without a customer value behind it is just a number someone liked.

Does the sale close on the website, or does it close on a call or after a quote? If it closes off site, we need to get that outcome back into Google, and that changes the build.

Where should the ads run, and is there an existing Google account with history, or is this starting from zero? An account with two years of conversion data is worth keeping. A fresh one is a different plan entirely.

On structure, we bill as a percentage of ad spend rather than hourly. It means we are not paid to be slow and not paid more for making the account complicated. I can put a specific number against it once I know the budget level.

Day to day the account would sit with a dedicated Google buyer on our team rather than being handed to a junior after signing, and you would get the search terms and the spend breakdown in writing whether or not the first month is flattering.
