## Draft

A young account with high CPC and high CPA is usually not being outbid. More often it is being starved, and the thing that matters is not how many months it has been running but how many conversions a month each campaign is actually feeding the bidding. In our experience accounts in this shape tend to sit below the volume where Smart Bidding can tell one click apart from the next, so bids stay volatile and settle high. The instinct to tighten, exact match only, heavy negatives, a narrow radius, is often what keeps them under that line. That is why "too restrictive" and "high CPC" usually turn out to be one problem rather than two.

So the first question is not which keywords are wasteful. It is whether the conversion signal being bid on is honest, and dense enough to bid on at all.

Roughly the order we would work in:

1. Conversion actions. Which are set to Primary and counted toward bidding, whether any fire on page load or on every phone tap, and whether one lead is being counted twice. A padded conversion teaches the algorithm to buy the wrong behavior, and it is the most common single cause of this exact pattern.
2. Change history on the bidding strategy. Switching between Maximize Conversions and Target CPA, or moving the target every couple of weeks, restarts learning each time. On an account this young that alone can explain unstable CPCs.
3. Conversions per campaign per month against the strategy in use. Target CPA on a campaign getting a handful a month is a coin flip that costs money.
4. Branded versus non branded, pulled apart. In a live account of ours last 90 days, the branded search campaign returns conversions at $56.79 while the non brand service campaign, same account and same tracking, returns them at $229.12. Blended, that account reads fine. Only one of those halves is buying new customers.
5. Search terms against actual lead quality, not the keyword list. Negatives come out of that comparison rather than a pasted generic list.
6. Impression share lost to rank versus lost to budget, and then the rank half split again. Rank loss from weak ad relevance is fixable. Rank loss because competitors are clearing a bid ceiling you cannot is a market answer, not a setup one, and those two get treated very differently.
7. Geography, hour of day, device, and whether search partners and display expansion are quietly on. At the conversion volume this account likely has, those splits are directional only, worth acting on when a pattern is large and obvious and worth ignoring when it is not.
8. Landing page to ad group match. A high CPC becomes survivable when conversion rate moves.

The deliverable is a written findings document: what is broken in tracking, what is structural, what is market, each recommendation ranked high, medium or low with the reasoning, and a short list of what to leave alone. If it needs a rebuild rather than a tune up, it says so plainly instead of selling twelve months of optimization on a broken foundation.

Closest example: our team took a bankruptcy practice in Orange County from 117 to 229 leads while cost per lead fell from $86 to $50.29, on spend that barely moved. That was restructuring around one market, not bid tinkering. Happy to send the write up.

A standalone audit runs $1,000 to $1,500 depending on account size. If management follows, it folds into onboarding rather than being charged twice.

Two things would sharpen this. Roughly what is the monthly spend, and when a lead is poor, is it the wrong service, outside your area, or the right person at a price they were never going to pay. Those are three different fixes and only one of them is a Google Ads problem.
