When an IMO disputes an appointment, what will you point to that says it was qualified? In appointment resale that definition decides your margin more than cost per lead does, and it has to exist before the first ad runs.

**The targeting constraint**

Annuity ads on Meta fall under the financial products special ad category, so you cannot target by age, gender or ZIP. For a product bought mostly by people approaching retirement, that means qualification has to move out of the targeting and into the funnel. The landing page and form carry the weight: age band, rough amount of money being repositioned, where it sits now, and timeline. Those answers then need to go back to Meta as their own event through the Conversions API, so the algorithm learns from qualified and booked appointments instead of from anyone willing to type a phone number.

**A comparable account**

I ran Meta for a reverse mortgage lender, also a restricted category with an older audience, where we moved optimization onto a prequalification event instead of the raw lead. The first nine days cost about $2,100 for one conversion. The next thirty active days came in near $107 across 66, and the seventy after that near $93 across 179. The event being counted changed underneath those numbers, so read it as what happens once Meta gets the right signal, not as a percentage improvement. The cheapest qualified leads came from retargeting people who opened the form and abandoned it.

**Compliance and what I would test**

Copy stays away from guaranteed-return and "safe as a CD" language and gets checked against the IMOs' own review before launch, since a rejected ad account costs more than a slow week. Testing runs on headlines and page angle (rollover worry, market-drop protection, income for life) measured by booked and showed appointments, reported every two weeks plus a live dashboard.

Is the monthly media budget under five thousand, between five and fifteen, or above that? It changes how many IMO buyers the volume can support at once.
