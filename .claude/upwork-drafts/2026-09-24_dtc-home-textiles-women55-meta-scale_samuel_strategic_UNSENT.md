The $1M+ a month bar first, since you asked for it plainly: our team has never run a single Meta account at that level. Our largest ran $90,000 to $115,000 a month for twelve straight months, and across all our accounts combined we run $198,000 to $266,000 a month on Meta. If $1M+ in one account is the line, we don't clear it. Same answer on Triple Whale and Northbeam, we haven't run an account on either, and we have no results to show on TikTok, YouTube or AppLovin.

What that book does show is what Q4 does to the auction. On that largest account, CPM went from $35.81 in October to $59.52 in November and $67.59 in December at flat spend, then back to $28.95 by March. Nothing in the account broke. A Q4 ramp lands in the same weeks the auction gets twice as expensive, so creative volume and CPA targets need to be set for December's CPM, not September's.

Case studies, and what we changed:

1. A replacement parts store on Meta, $11,600 to $15,600 a month. In January, the month before we took it over, cost per purchase was $34.72. We cut it from 11 active campaigns to 7, retired six small inherited ones and fixed the targeting our audit flagged. By April cost per purchase was $19.89, and it was $22.91 in May. CPM sat around $8 throughout, so traffic didn't get cheaper. The audit also changed creative, so we can't isolate which change did the most, and we won't claim one did.

2. A premium furniture brand on Meta, about $105,000 over four months, peaking at $40,389 in July. The July headline was 4.17x Meta-reported ROAS, and here is what was under it: 59% of spend went to a 50% off sitewide sale, and a retargeting campaign took 19% of spend and reported 35% of revenue at 7.9x. That retargeting number is exactly the kind to check in your attribution tool before moving budget into it, because many of those buyers were already coming back.

That is the middle of funnel problem at your spend too. Retargeting that caps out is usually showing the same winning ads to people who already said no to them. We split warm pools by what they did (watched most of a video, visited without adding to cart, added to cart without buying), give each a different reason to buy (reviews from women their age, fabric and care close-ups, the returns promise), exclude recent purchasers, and judge it on new-customer revenue in your attribution tool, with a holdout, before it gets more budget.

Where we're strongest: separating what the account did from what the auction did before touching budgets, cost caps to scale spend at a CPA ceiling, and bid caps in weeks when CPM spikes and not spending beats overpaying.

The ad fatiguing at $50k a day, next 7 days:

Day 1: Confirm it's fatigue. If CPM rose across the whole account, it's the auction and cutting the ad won't fix it. Fatigue looks like rising frequency and falling click-through on this ad while account CPM holds. Check whether the drop sits in one age band or placement.
Day 1: Don't kill it. Move it under a cost cap at target CPA so it only spends where it still clears.
Day 2: Brief the strategists on 4 to 6 iterations of the same concept: new first three seconds, new opening frame, a different woman telling the story, static and carousel cuts. The concept won, the execution wore out.
Day 3: People who watched the fatigued ad get a different ad in retargeting, not the same one again.
Days 3 to 4: Launch iterations in the testing campaign at a fixed daily budget. Kill anything that spends 1.5x target CPA without a purchase.
Days 5 to 6: Winners go into a new ad set in the scaling campaign, not the fatiguing one, since adding an ad to a live ad set restarts learning.
Day 7: Compare new-customer CPA in your attribution tool against Meta, set the old ad at whatever budget it still clears, and write up for the creative team what died, what held and what to iterate next.

The goal for the week is three or four ads carrying the $50k, not one replacement.
